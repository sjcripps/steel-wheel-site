Railcar Storage Rates: What It Costs to Park Idle Cars (2026)

October 3, 2026 · 13 min read · Equipment
The cheapest stored car is one that is moving freight again. Get an indicative rail freight quote for your lane in seconds — free, no signup.
The short version: Off-railroad storage yards publish ranges of about $4 to $16 per car per day, plus roughly $100 to $300 per car to switch each car in and again to switch it out (RSI Logistics published ranges). Leaving private empties on a Class I railroad's track is usually the most expensive option. And storage is never the whole bill: the lease payment keeps running while the car sits. Idle cars cost $4–16 a day to store, or you can list them free on the sublease board and let them earn rent.

Every fleet manager eventually has the same problem: cars on lease, a payment that arrives every month, and no freight for the cars to carry. Harvest ends, a plant goes down for a rebuild, a customer pulls a contract, or the market just goes quiet. The cars have to go somewhere. Where you park them, and how long you leave them there, can easily cost more than the lease itself. In February 2026 about 21.8% of the North American fleet, roughly 356,000 cars, was in storage (AAR Rail Industry Overview). Plenty of other shippers are paying this bill too.

This guide covers what railcar storage actually costs, the four places idle cars usually end up, how to pick a yard, and the point at which storing a car stops making sense and you should sublease it, return it or sell it.

Railcar Storage Rates in 2026

Railcar storage at an off-railroad yard typically costs $4 to $16 per car per day. Add a switch charge of about $100 to $300 per car to place the car on the storage track and the same again to pull it. Those are published market ranges from RSI Logistics, not quotes. Every yard prices on its own tariff, and the same yard will quote different numbers for different cars.

Convert the daily rate to a monthly figure and you see why storage deserves attention. At $4 a day a car costs about $120 a month to store. At $16 a day it costs about $480 a month. Grain-service covered hoppers have been reported leasing at $530 to $635 a month (Grain Journal, 2025; see our railcar lease rates tracker). At the top of the storage band you are paying almost a second lease payment to park the car.

What moves a car up or down the range

  • Empty vs. loaded. Clean empties are the cheapest cars to store. Many yards will not take loaded cars, and the ones that do charge more because a loaded car carries more liability and more weight on the track.
  • Hazmat and residue. A tank car that last carried a hazardous material and has not been cleaned is still regulated as hazmat packaging. Fewer yards accept residue cars, and they price accordingly. Cars in toxic-by-inhalation (TIH) service have the fewest options of all.
  • Term and block size. Yards want long, predictable stays. Fifty cars for six months will price better per car per day than five cars for three weeks.
  • Switching frequency. If you will be pulling cars a few at a time as demand comes back, expect to pay for every pull. Bulk in and bulk out is cheaper.
  • Season and location. When a whole sector parks cars at once, track near the busy corridors fills up and prices move with it.

The True Cost of an Idle Car

The storage invoice is the smallest part of what an idle car costs. The full cost is the lease payment plus storage plus switching plus the empty moves to get the car to the yard and back to a loading point. Shippers who compare only the storage rates between yards are optimizing the wrong line.

Here is an illustrative example. Twenty leased covered hoppers at $580 per car per month go idle for five months (about 150 days) and sit at a short-line yard charging $8 per car per day.

Lease payments while idle20 cars × $580 × 5 months$58,000

That $90,000 buys nothing except the right to have the cars back later. It is the number any alternative has to beat. A sublease that brings in even $400 per car per month over the same five months returns $40,000 and eliminates the $32,000 in storage and switching, a $72,000 swing on a 20-car fleet. That is why the decision in the last section of this guide matters more than which yard has the lowest daily rate.

Where to Park Idle Cars: Four Options

Idle cars usually end up in one of four places: your own track, a short-line or regional railroad's storage track, a contract storage yard, or a Class I railroad's track under its private-car storage rules. Each has a different cost profile.

OptionTypical costBest forWatch out for
Your own plant track or sidingNo storage fee; switching still appliesShort gaps, small car countsBlocks track you need for loading and receiving
Short-line or regional railroad storageWithin the $4–16/day bandSeasonal and multi-month storageConnection to the railroad you need when cars come back
Contract storage yard or terminalWithin the $4–16/day bandCars that need cleaning, repairs or inspection during the stayMinimum terms, separate charges for added services
Class I private-car storageUsually the highestShort holds when nothing else is availableDaily charges that keep accruing until the car moves

Your own track

If you have spare track at the plant, parking cars there costs nothing in storage fees. The catch is that idle cars take up track you use to stage loads and receive inbound cars. If they slow down your loading, the savings disappear. This option works for a handful of cars for a few weeks, not a fleet for a season.

Short-line and regional railroads

Many short lines market storage track as a revenue line. They have light traffic, long sidings and every reason to fill them. Several Class I railroads publish short-line storage directories that list each line's capacity, contacts and whether it accepts hazmat. For seasonal storage this is usually the best balance of price and access.

Contract storage yards

Terminal operators and car-service companies run dedicated storage yards. Many of them also clean, repair and inspect cars, which matters if the cars need work before their next assignment anyway. Doing that work while the car sits saves a separate shop trip later.

Class I railroad track

Each Class I bills private empty cars held on its track under its own storage program or tariff. It is convenient, because the car never leaves the network. It is also usually the most expensive place to leave a car, and the charges accrue whether or not anyone is paying attention. Treat this as a short-term holding pattern while you arrange real storage.

Storage vs. Demurrage: Not the Same Bill

Storage and demurrage both charge you for time, but they apply to different situations. Demurrage is what a railroad bills when a car sits at your facility, or is held for you, beyond the free time in its tariff. It mostly hits cars that are being loaded or unloaded. Storage is what you pay to park cars, usually private empties, that have no job at the moment.

The two overlap when cars that should be in storage are left sitting at a customer facility or on railroad track. Then you pay demurrage or private-car storage rates for what should have been cheap yard storage. If you are seeing large demurrage bills on cars that are not actually in service, that is usually a storage problem. How to Choose a Storage Yard

The best storage yard is the one that keeps the total cost low, which means the daily rate, the empty moves on either end and the time it takes to get the cars back into service. Work through these in order:

  1. Start from where the cars go next. A cheap yard far from your next loading point costs you on the empty move back. Pick a yard close to where demand will come back, not close to where the cars went idle.
  2. Confirm the railroads served. The yard has to connect to the railroad that will move the car out. A short line that interchanges only with the wrong carrier adds a handoff and a charge to every car.
  3. Match the car to the yard's rules. Ask directly about loaded cars, hazmat residue, TIH and car length. Get the answer in writing before the cars move.
  4. Ask about switching lead time. When demand returns you need cars out in days, not weeks. Ask how often the yard switches and how much notice it needs to pull a block.
  5. Read the contract terms. Look for the minimum stay, the notice period, how switching is billed, who carries liability for damage or theft, and what the yard does with cars when the contract ends.
  6. Check security. Remote storage track invites graffiti and component theft. Fenced or monitored yards cost more but cost less than replacing parts.

What Happens to a Car While It Sits

A stored car is not frozen in time. Lease obligations, inspection due dates and the car's age all keep running while it sits. Plan for these before the cars go in, not when you need them back:

  • Qualification and inspection dates. Tank cars carry requalification due dates that keep running in storage. A car that ages past its date while parked needs shop time before it can carry product again.
  • Return-to-service work. Cars stored for long periods often need an inspection before they go back into service, and sometimes brake or running-gear work. Budget for it so you are not surprised when the cars come out.
  • Lease clauses. Your lease probably says who pays storage, who is liable for damage in storage and whether you can store at all without notice. Many full-service leases still leave storage and its risks with the lessee. If you are unsure what yours covers, our guide to full-service vs. net railcar leases explains where the lines usually fall.
  • Contamination. Cars in food-grade or sensitive service can pick up debris, moisture or pests while parked. Expect to clean or inspect them before they load again.

Store, Sublease, Return or Sell?

Storage is the right answer when the idle period is short and you know the cars will be needed again. When the idle period stretches to months, storage is usually the most expensive option because you are paying the lease and the yard at the same time. Use the expected idle period to pick the option:

Idle periodUsually the best moveWhy
A few weeksStore (own track or nearby yard)A sublease costs more to arrange than the weeks of storage it would save
Two months or moreSubleaseRent offsets the lease payment and storage disappears; most leases allow it with the lessor's written consent
Near the end of the leaseNegotiate an early return or a reduced fleet at renewalPaying storage on cars you will not renew rarely makes sense
Owned cars with no long-term jobSell, or lease the cars outA car in storage is capital tied up in equipment that keeps aging

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does railcar storage cost per day?

Published market ranges run about $4 to $16 per car per day at off-railroad storage yards, plus roughly $100 to $300 per car to switch each car in and again to switch it out. The rate depends on car type, whether the car is loaded or carries hazmat residue, the term and the number of cars. Treat these as planning ranges, not quotes.

Where can I store idle railcars?

The usual options are your own plant track, a short-line or regional railroad's storage track, a contract storage yard run by a terminal or car-service company, or a Class I railroad's track under its private-car storage rules. Short lines and contract yards are usually the best value for multi-month storage. Class I track is usually the most expensive.

Is railcar storage the same as demurrage?

No. Demurrage is charged when a car sits at your facility or is held for you beyond the railroad's free time, usually during loading or unloading. Storage is the charge for parking cars, usually private empties, that have no current assignment. Leaving idle cars on railroad track can mean paying demurrage or private-car storage instead of cheaper yard storage.

Is it cheaper to sublease idle railcars than to store them?

Usually, once the idle period runs past a couple of months. A stored car costs the lease payment plus storage and switching. A subleased car earns rent that offsets the lease and removes the storage bill. Most master leases allow subleasing with the lessor's written consent. For idle periods of a few weeks, storage is usually simpler.

Do loaded railcars cost more to store?

Yes. Many storage yards accept only empty cars, and the ones that take loaded cars charge more because of the added liability and track load. Cars carrying hazmat residue have fewer yard options and higher rates, and cars in toxic-by-inhalation service have the fewest options of all.
