Part A

The Economics of Transloading

When does rail + truck beat truck alone?

What Is Transloading?

  • Transferring freight from one mode (rail) to another (truck) at an intermediate facility
  • Bridges the gap between rail network and final delivery point
  • The shipper or receiver doesn't need their own rail siding
  • Transload facility provides: track, equipment, storage, truck loading/unloading
  • Used for both inbound (rail to truck to customer) and outbound (truck to rail for long haul)
  • Common at ports, distribution hubs, and industrial parks near rail lines
  • Growing rapidly as shippers who lost direct rail service (PSR closures) need alternatives
  • A well-run transload operation can save 30-50% vs. all-truck for loads over 300 miles

The Transload Math: Rail + Truck vs

  • Rail Cost. $0.04/ton-mi
  • Long-haul rail rate. 500 mile example: $20/ton
  • Efficient for distance
  • Need 25+ ton loads
  • Transload Fee. $15-$35/ton
  • Handling at facility
  • Varies by commodity
  • Includes storage (limited)
  • Equipment + labor
  • Last-Mile Truck. $3-$5/mi
  • Transload to customer
  • Typically 25-75 miles
  • Example: 50 mi = $175-$250
  • Per truck, not per ton
  • All-Truck Alternative. $0.12-$0.20/ton-mi
  • Full distance by truck. 500 mi = $60-$100/ton
  • Simpler logistics
  • But much more expensive

When Transloading Makes Sense (and When It Doesn't)

Good Fit. Distance > 300 miles (origin to final destination). Heavy, bulk commodities (high $/ton-mile by truck). No rail siding at receiver but rail access nearby. Consistent, predictable volumes (weekly/monthly). Commodity can be handled at transload (not fragile). Existing transload facility on the route. Environmental/ESG mandate to reduce truck emissions. Not a Good Fit. Distance < 200 miles (truck wins on simplicity). Small, infrequent volumes (can't fill a railcar). Time-sensitive delivery (rail adds 2-5 days). Commodity requires special handling equipment. No transload facility near destination. Receiver needs just-in-time delivery. Product degrades with double handling.

Dry Bulk Transloading Operations

  • AGGREGATES (sand, gravel, crushed stone): Dump pit + conveyor to stockpile + truck loading
  • GRAIN: Rail hopper to grain leg/elevator + storage bin + truck loadout or barge loading
  • CEMENT/FLY ASH: Pneumatic unloading from hopper to silo + pneumatic truck loading
  • PLASTIC PELLETS: Hopper to silo (pneumatic) + truck loadout; contamination control critical
  • FERTILIZER: Hopper to storage pad or building + front-end loader to truck
  • Key equipment: conveyor systems, front-end loaders, pneumatic blowers, scales
  • Throughput: 4-12 railcars/day typical for a mid-size facility
  • Storage capacity determines how many cars you can handle between truck pickups

Liquid Bulk Transloading Operations

  • CHEMICALS: Tank car to storage tank + truck tanker loading; containment + safety systems required
  • FUELS (diesel, gasoline, ethanol): Tank car to AST + truck rack loading; vapor recovery required
  • FOOD-GRADE LIQUIDS: Dedicated tank cars + dedicated storage; FDA compliance, kosher/halal certs
  • Key equipment: pumps, hoses, loading arms, vapor recovery, containment berms, fire suppression
  • Metering and measurement: flow meters, scales, tank gauging for custody transfer accuracy
  • Environmental requirements: SPCC plan, containment for 110% of largest tank, stormwater management
  • Throughput: 2-8 tank cars/day depending on product viscosity and pump capacity
  • Heated/insulated products (asphalt, sulfur): require steam or electric heating infrastructure

Transload Facility Layout: Key Components

  • Track capacity: 5-20+ car spots
  • Lead track connection to mainline
  • Car spotter or locomotive
  • Unloading equipment positioned
  • Track scale (if weight critical)
  • Adequate clearance for equipment. 🏭
  • Operations Area
  • Storage: silos, tanks, or pads
  • Covered storage for weather-sensitive
  • Processing equipment (crushing, blending)
  • Scales for truck weighing
  • Office/dispatch building
  • Maintenance shop for equipment. 🚛
  • Truck loading lanes (2-4 typical)
  • Queuing area for waiting trucks
  • Weigh-in and weigh-out scales
  • Truck wash (if contamination control)
  • Security gate / access control
  • Exit road to highway access

Finding Existing Transload Facilities

  • Steel Wheel Logistics Transload Directory — our own directory of transload facilities nationwide, searchable by state and city
  • CSX TRANSFLO — CSX's own transload network, ~100 facilities nationwide
  • OmniTRAX terminal services — operates transloads on its short-line network
  • Watco — transload and terminal services across 40+ states
  • Genesee & Wyoming terminal services — operates transloads on G&W short lines
  • Railroad sales reps — ask your Class I or short-line for transload options near your destination
  • State economic development agencies — often maintain lists of rail-served industrial sites
  • Look for facilities within 50-75 miles of your final destination for optimal last-mile truck cost
Part B

Starting a Transload Operation

Site selection, equipment, permitting, and railroad agreements

Starting a Transload Operation: Key Steps

  • STEP 1: Site selection — rail-served land with highway access, zoning, and utilities
  • STEP 2: Railroad agreement — industry track agreement or sidetrack agreement with serving carrier
  • STEP 3: Permitting — environmental (SPCC, stormwater), building, zoning, fire department
  • STEP 4: Track construction — turnout, lead track, spur track(s) for car spots
  • STEP 5: Equipment procurement — conveyor, pneumatic, pumps, scales, loader, containers
  • STEP 6: Storage infrastructure — silos, tanks, pads, covered buildings as needed
  • STEP 7: Operations setup — staffing, SOPs, safety training, truck scheduling system
  • Capital investment: $500K-$5M+ depending on commodity and scale

Railroad Agreements for Transloaders

  • Industry Track Agreement: railroad builds/maintains the track; you pay via freight rates
  • Private Sidetrack Agreement: you build and maintain your own track; lower freight rates
  • Switching charges: $200-$600 per car for railroad to spot/pull from your facility
  • Minimum annual volume commitments (railroads won't build track for 5 cars/year)
  • Insurance requirements: $5M+ general liability is standard
  • Indemnification: you'll indemnify the railroad for incidents on your property
  • Access: railroad needs 24/7 access to spot and pull cars
  • CRITICAL: get the agreement in writing before investing in infrastructure

📋 Practical Exercise: Transload Feasibility Analysis

  • Scenario: Your company ships 100 truckloads/month of construction aggregates. from a quarry in central Alabama to a job site in metro Atlanta (250 miles)
  • Current cost: $185/truckload (25 tons) = $7.40/ton by truck alone. 1
  • Research: Is there a transload facility near Atlanta for aggregates?. 2
  • Estimate rail cost: quarry to transload (use $0.04/ton-mile x rail distance). 3
  • Estimate transload fee: $15-$20/ton for aggregates. 4
  • Estimate last-mile truck: transload to jobsite (distance x $3.50/mi / 25 tons). 5
  • Calculate total rail+transload+truck cost per ton. 6
  • Compare to current all-truck cost ($7.40/ton). 7
  • What's the monthly savings? What's the annual savings?. 8
  • At what distance does the rail option break even vs. truck?

STUDY GUIDE

Module 5 Review. Key Terms • Review Questions • Practical Assignments.

Key Terms

Transloading. Transferring freight between transportation modes (rail to truck). Transload Facility. Site with rail and truck access plus equipment for freight transfer. Industry Track. Railroad-owned track serving an industrial customer's facility. Private Sidetrack. Customer-owned track connected to the railroad network. TRANSFLO. CSX's transloading network; ~100 facilities nationwide. SPCC Plan. Spill Prevention Control & Countermeasure plan (EPA requirement). Constructive Placement. Car billed as 'placed' even if held at a nearby yard. Last-Mile Delivery. Final truck leg from transload facility to customer. Custody Transfer. Point where ownership/liability shifts between parties.

Practical Assignment

  • Step 1: Identify your top 3 freight lanes by volume and cost
  • Step 2: For each lane, research transload facility options near the destination (use our transload directory)
  • Step 3: Calculate the rail + transload + truck cost for each lane
  • Step 4: Compare to your current all-truck cost and calculate potential savings
  • Step 5: If no suitable transload exists, estimate the cost to build one (site, track, equipment)
  • Step 6: Prepare a feasibility summary: savings potential, capital required, payback period
  • Step 7: Contact at least one transload operator for a rate quote on your commodity

Key Takeaways

✅ Transloading extends rail's reach to customers without rail sidings. ✅ The math works for distances over 300 miles and bulk commodities. ✅ Rail + transload + truck can save 30-50% vs. all-truck for qualifying freight. ✅ Existing transload facilities are available — check our transload directory, TRANSFLO, railroad reps. ✅ Starting a new transload: $500K-$5M+ capital, plus railroad agreement and permits. ✅ Environmental compliance is non-negotiable, especially for liquids and chemicals. ✅ Transloading is growing as PSR reduces direct rail service to smaller shippers. ✅ The key variables: distance, commodity, transload fee, and last-mile truck cost.
