Rail Freight Shipping: How It Works, Step by Step

Updated September 24, 2026 · 12 min read · Fundamentals
Key fact: Rail freight shipping moves roughly 40% of U.S. long-distance freight by ton-miles on about 140,000 route miles. A single carload carries what three to four trucks carry, and rail's line-haul cost runs about 2 to 4 cents per ton-mile against 15 to 25 cents for truck.

Rail freight shipping is not booking a truck with more wheels. It has its own equipment, its own paperwork, its own pricing basis (per car, not per ton) and its own way of moving your load through a network of yards and interchanges. This guide walks the whole process end to end: who the players are, the three ways freight moves by rail, the eight steps from quote to empty release, how to ship without a rail siding, what it costs, and how to tell whether your lane belongs on rail at all.

What Rail Freight Shipping Is (and Who It Is For)

Rail freight shipping is the movement of goods in railcars over the North American freight rail network. Six Class I freight railroads and more than 600 short lines operate that network as a connected system: a car loaded on a short line in one corner of the continent can be interchanged onto a Class I, handed to a second Class I, and delivered by another short line at the far end, all under one bill of lading.

Rail fits a specific kind of freight. The sweet spot is heavy, dense or bulk product moving a long distance on a repeating basis: grain, fertilizer, aggregates, cement, steel, lumber, plastics, chemicals, petroleum products, paper and scrap. If you can fill a car (roughly 70 to 110 net tons depending on the car type) and you ship the same lane more than a few times a year, rail deserves a serious look. If you ship pallets on demand to a customer 150 miles away, it does not.

What rail buys you is a lower cost per ton on long hauls, capacity that does not depend on finding 50 drivers, and a carbon footprint about a quarter of trucking's. What it asks in return is planning: cars are ordered in advance, loading happens inside a free-time window, and the load travels on the railroad's schedule rather than yours.

The Players You Need to Know

Understanding who does what saves you weeks of confusion on your first move.

  • Class I railroads — BNSF, Union Pacific, CSX, Norfolk Southern, CN and CPKC. They own the main lines, publish the tariffs and handle the long-haul move. Our short line vs Class I guide lists all six and what each one serves.
  • Short line and regional railroads — More than 600 smaller carriers that handle the first and last rail mile. They pull cars from shipper sidings, deliver them to a Class I interchange, and do the reverse at destination. Many industrial sidings are on a short line, not a Class I.
  • Rail logistics providers — Companies like Steel Wheel Logistics that act as a shipper's outsourced rail department: pricing lanes against the tariffs, ordering cars, coordinating transload, tracking, and auditing the invoices. If you do not ship enough volume to get a Class I's attention, this is how you get rail-department expertise without hiring one.
  • Transload and rail-served terminals — Facilities where freight transfers between truck and railcar. Most shippers do not have their own track, so a transload facility or rail-served warehouse is how their freight gets on and off the train.
  • Railcar lessors — Companies that lease cars to shippers who want guaranteed equipment. Railroad-supplied cars are often short in peak season, so many regular shippers lease their own fleet. See our guide to railcar leasing companies.

Carload, Unit Train and Multimodal: Three Ways Freight Moves by Rail

Carload (manifest service)

One or a few cars from your facility travel with cars from other shippers, sorted into trains at classification yards along the way. You can ship a single car. It is the most flexible rail service and the one most industrial shippers use.

Unit train

A whole train, typically 75 to 120 cars, of one commodity from one origin to one destination. It bypasses classification yards, so it moves faster and costs less per ton. It requires serious volume and loading facilities that can fill a train quickly. Grain, coal, crude, ethanol and frac sand move this way.

Multimodal

Truck on one or both ends, rail in the middle. Freight is trucked to a transload or rail-served terminal, rides rail for the long haul, and is trucked to the final door. This is how most shippers without a siding use rail, and it is also how containers move by rail.

We compare the first two in more depth in unit trains vs manifest service. The short version: if you can fill a train, unit-train pricing is hard to beat; if you cannot, carload is your service and the rest of this guide is about making it work well.

The Rail Freight Shipping Process, Step by Step

Here is what actually happens between deciding to ship by rail and releasing the empty car back to the railroad.

1

Confirm the lane fits rail

Before you price anything, check three things: is the origin rail-served or near a transload terminal; is the destination rail-served or near one; and is the volume enough to justify the planning rail requires. A lane that fails one of the three can still work, but it will cost more, and you should know that before you get a quote. Our guide on evaluating whether rail makes sense walks the decision.

2

Get a rate

Rail is priced per car, not per ton or per mile. A rate quote needs the commodity (ideally its STCC code), the car type, origin and destination stations, annual or monthly volume, and who supplies the car. Every Class I publishes tariff rates you can price against, and shippers with enough volume negotiate contract rates below tariff. Fuel surcharge is added on top and changes monthly. A rail logistics provider prices your lane across every routing option and tells you where the number comes from. Our rail shipping quote guide lists exactly what to have ready.

3

Choose equipment and order the car

The car has to match the commodity: covered hoppers for grain, cement and pellets; open hoppers and gondolas for aggregates and scrap; tank cars for liquids; boxcars for packaged and paper products; flatcars and centerbeams for lumber, steel and oversized loads. Our guide to the types of rail cars covers capacities and which fits what. Once the type is set, you place a car order with the serving railroad (or the lessor supplies your leased car) and the railroad spots the empty at your siding or terminal.

4

Load and secure

You load inside the railroad's free-time window, usually measured in days from the spot. Bulk cars load by gravity, conveyor or pneumatic transfer. Open-top and flatcar loads must be blocked and braced to AAR loading rules, and a poorly secured load is the single most common reason a car gets pulled from a train. Weigh the car: overloading beyond the car's gross rail load limit will stop it at the first scale.

5

Bill it: the bill of lading and shipping instructions

Rail moves on a bill of lading submitted electronically to the origin railroad. It carries the shipper, consignee, care-of party, commodity and STCC, weight, car number, route and who pays. Get the route and the paying party right: the bill of lading is what the railroad rates the move from, and a mistake here becomes a billing dispute later. Hazardous materials add 49 CFR 172 requirements and an emergency response contact.

6

Switching, classification and interchange

This is where rail differs most from trucking. A local crew pulls your loaded car and takes it to a classification yard, where it is sorted with other cars heading the same direction and coupled into a road train. If the move involves more than one railroad, the car is handed off at an interchange point and re-sorted on the receiving carrier. Each yard touch and each interchange adds handling and dwell, which is why the number of railroads on your route matters as much as the mileage.

7

Transit and tracking

Every car carries an AEI tag read by trackside scanners across the network, so its location updates as it passes each reader. Railroads report those events as car location messages, which you can see in the carrier's shipper portal or through your logistics provider. Watch for a car that stops updating for more than a day; that usually means it is sitting in a yard waiting for a connection, and a phone call can move it.

8

Delivery, unloading and empty release

At destination the car is spotted at the receiver's siding or transload terminal. The receiver unloads inside its free-time window, then releases the empty back to the railroad. Miss the window and you pay demurrage by the day, which is the accessorial that surprises new shippers most. The railroad's freight bill follows and should be audited line by line against the quoted rate, fuel surcharge and any accessorials.

Shipping by Rail Without a Rail Siding

Most businesses do not have track on their property, and that is not a barrier. Freight moves by truck to a rail-served terminal, transfers into a railcar, rides rail for the long haul, and transfers back to truck at the far end if the receiver is not rail-served either. Three ways to do it:

  • Transload terminal — A public facility with track, equipment and yard space that transfers your product between truck and railcar for a per-ton or per-car fee. Our transload directory lists facilities by state and commodity, and what is transloading explains the fees and how they work.
  • Rail-served warehouse — A third-party warehouse with a siding that receives railcars, stores the product and ships it out by truck on demand. Good for finished goods and packaged product that needs staging near the customer. See our rail-served warehouse directory.
  • Your own spur — A private track connecting your facility to the serving railroad. It removes the transload step for good but is a capital project; our guide on private rail spur cost covers what to expect.

Transload adds a handling fee and a drayage leg, so it pays off on longer hauls and heavier loads. On a short lane, the extra step can erase rail's line-haul advantage, which is one of the first things a lane evaluation checks.

What Rail Freight Shipping Costs

The basis is per car. A carload rate is quoted for a specific commodity, car type, origin and destination, and it covers the line haul only. On top of it come:

  • Fuel surcharge — A percentage or per-mile amount tied to a published fuel index, reset monthly. Our guide explains how rail fuel surcharges are calculated.
  • Accessorials — Switching, weighing, diversion, storage and demurrage. Most are avoidable with planning; demurrage is the one to watch.
  • Transload and drayage — If either end is off-rail, the terminal fee and the truck leg are part of your true cost.
  • Equipment — Railroad-supplied cars are built into the rate; private or leased cars usually earn a lower line-haul rate but carry a lease cost.

As a rule of thumb, rail line haul runs about 2 to 4 cents per ton-mile against 15 to 25 cents for truck, and a carload of bulk product on a long lane typically lands in the low-to-mid thousands of dollars per car. Distance is not linear: short hauls carry a minimum charge, and the per-mile cost falls as mileage rises. The full breakdown, with a worked example and current ranges by commodity, is in our rail freight rates guide. How Long Rail Freight Shipping Takes

Rail is slower than a dedicated truck, and anyone who quotes you a transit time without knowing your exact origin, destination and routing is guessing. What actually sets transit on a carload move:

  • Distance and route — The obvious one, but the route the car takes is not always the shortest line on the map.
  • Number of railroads — Every interchange is a handoff and a re-sort. A single-line move is faster and more predictable than a move that touches three carriers.
  • Yard touches — Each classification yard the car passes through adds dwell. Unit trains skip the yards, which is most of why they are faster.
  • Service frequency — Some short lines and branch lines are served a few days a week. If your car is ready the day after the local runs, it waits.
  • Loading and release — Free time at both ends is inside the door-to-door clock. Slow unloading at the receiver delays the empty and the next load.

The right answer is lane-specific: ask the serving railroad or your logistics provider for the published service schedule on your exact routing, then plan inventory around that rather than around a rule of thumb.

Rail wins on cost when the haul is long, the load is heavy and the lane repeats. Truck wins on speed, flexibility and door-to-door service on shorter hauls and smaller quantities. The break-even depends on the lane, the commodity, and whether either end needs a transload; we run the math with real numbers in our rail vs truck freight cost comparison. For many shippers the answer is both: rail for the long haul, truck for the ends. That is multimodal shipping, and it captures most of rail's cost advantage without giving up truck's flexibility.

Mistakes First-Time Rail Shippers Make

  • Pricing rail like truck. Asking for a per-ton rate and comparing it to a truck rate without the transload, drayage and fuel surcharge on the rail side gives you a comparison that falls apart on the first invoice.
  • Ordering the wrong car. A commodity in the wrong car type is either refused, loaded light, or damaged in transit. Match the car to the product before you order.
  • Ignoring free time. Demurrage is billed per car per day and accumulates quietly. Know the free time at both ends and staff loading and unloading to hit it.
  • Getting the bill of lading wrong. Wrong consignee, wrong route or wrong paying party turns a clean move into a billing dispute.
  • Not auditing the freight bill. Rail invoices carry the base rate, fuel surcharge and accessorials as separate lines. Check every one against the quote.

We cover more of these, with what to do instead, in common mistakes new rail shippers make and shipping by rail for the first time.

Getting Started

If you ship everything by truck today and want to know whether rail belongs in the mix, here is the honest test: rail works for consistent, heavy or bulk freight on long lanes. Ten pallets to a distribution center a couple of hours away should stay on a truck. Two thousand tons of aggregate a month from a quarry to a terminal several states away should be on rail, and probably already would be if the planning were handled for you.

The first step is a lane evaluation: origin and destination rail access, volume, commodity and car type, priced against the carriers' tariffs and the transload options on both ends. Frequently Asked Questions

How does rail freight shipping work?

A shipper gets a per-car rate for a commodity and lane, orders a railcar, loads it at a rail siding or a transload terminal, and submits a bill of lading to the origin railroad. The railroad pulls the car, sorts it through classification yards, interchanges it to other carriers if the route requires, and delivers it to the receiver's siding or terminal. The receiver unloads inside the free-time window and releases the empty car.

What is the difference between carload and multimodal rail shipping?

Carload service moves your product in a dedicated railcar from a rail-served origin to a rail-served destination. Multimodal shipping uses truck on one or both ends, with rail in the middle, so shippers without a siding can still use rail. Container moves are a form of multimodal shipping.

Can I ship by rail if my facility has no rail siding?

Yes. Truck the product to a transload terminal or rail-served warehouse, where it is transferred into a railcar for the long haul. Most rail shipments start or end this way. The transload fee and drayage leg are part of your cost, so the economics work best on longer, heavier lanes.

How much does it cost to ship by rail?

Rail is priced per car. Line-haul cost runs roughly 2 to 4 cents per ton-mile, and a carload of bulk product on a long lane typically lands in the low-to-mid thousands of dollars per car before fuel surcharge, accessorials and any transload or drayage. Our rail freight rates guide breaks down each component, and our quote tool gives an indicative number for a specific lane.

How long does rail freight shipping take?

It depends on the distance, the number of railroads and classification yards on the route, how often the origin and destination are served, and loading and unloading time at each end. Single-line moves are faster and more predictable than moves that interchange between carriers. Ask for the published service schedule on your exact routing rather than relying on a general figure.

What does a rail logistics provider do?

A rail logistics provider works as a shipper's outsourced rail department. It prices lanes against the railroads' tariffs and negotiates where volume allows, orders cars and sources leased equipment when railroad cars are short, arranges transload and drayage, tracks cars in transit, and audits freight bills for fuel surcharge and accessorial errors. Shippers use one to get rail expertise without hiring for it.

What is a unit train?

A unit train is a whole train of one commodity moving from one origin to one destination without being broken up in classification yards. It moves faster and costs less per ton than carload service, but it requires enough volume to fill 75 to 120 cars at once and facilities that can load and unload a train quickly.

What is demurrage in rail shipping?

Demurrage is a daily charge the railroad bills when a car is held at a siding or terminal beyond the free time allowed for loading or unloading. It is the accessorial that surprises new rail shippers most, and it is avoidable by knowing the free-time rules at both ends and staffing loading and unloading to meet them.

Is rail cheaper than truck?

On long hauls with heavy or bulk freight, rail's line-haul cost per ton is usually well below truck's. On short hauls, or when both ends need a transload and drayage, truck can be cheaper door to door. The break-even is lane-specific and depends on distance, commodity, volume and rail access at each end.

How do I track a railcar in transit?

Every railcar carries an AEI tag that trackside readers scan as the car moves across the network. The railroad turns those reads into car location messages, which you can view in the carrier's shipper portal or through a rail logistics provider that consolidates tracking across carriers.
