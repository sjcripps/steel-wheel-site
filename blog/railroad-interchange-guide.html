Railroad Interchange Guide: How Freight Moves Between Carriers

April 7, 2026 · 10 min read · Operations
Key fact: Most rail shipments that travel more than a few hundred miles pass through at least one interchange point, where one railroad hands the car off to another. How smoothly that handoff goes has a direct impact on your transit time, your freight's condition, and your cost.

If you've ever tracked a rail shipment and watched it sit motionless for two days at a point that isn't origin or destination, you've experienced the reality of railroad interchange. It's the part of rail freight that shippers hear the least about — and the part that causes some of the most frustrating delays. Understanding how interchange works, where the friction points are, and what you can do about them puts you in a much stronger position to manage your supply chain.

What Is Railroad Interchange?

Railroad interchange is the transfer of freight cars from one railroad to another. When a shipment's origin and destination are on different railroads' networks — which is the case for a large share of all rail freight — the car has to physically move from one carrier's tracks to another's. That handoff point is the interchange.

The concept is straightforward, but the execution involves a surprising amount of coordination. Each interchange requires a physical inspection of the car, a transfer of paperwork and electronic data, a shift in liability from one carrier to the next, and — in many cases — a reclassification of the car into a new outbound train. Every one of those steps takes time, and any one of them can go wrong.

For shippers, interchange is the single biggest variable in rail transit consistency. A shipment that stays on one carrier's network from origin to destination (a "single-line" move) is almost always more predictable than one that crosses two or three railroads. That doesn't mean multi-carrier moves are bad — they're often the only option, and they can be cost-effective — but understanding the interchange process helps you plan around it.

Why Interchange Exists

The North American rail network isn't one unified system. It's a patchwork of roughly 600 railroads, from the seven Class I carriers that operate the long-haul mainlines down to hundreds of short line and regional railroads that serve local industries and branch lines. No single railroad reaches everywhere, so freight that needs to move across the network has to pass between carriers.

This fragmented structure is a product of history. Railroads were built by hundreds of different companies over the course of a century, each with their own track gauge preferences, operating territories, and corporate interests. Consolidation merged many of them into today's Class I carriers, but the network still has clear seams. BNSF dominates the western half of the country. Norfolk Southern and CSX split the east. Canadian National and Canadian Pacific Kansas City bridge the US-Canada border. And short lines fill in the gaps everywhere.

The result: a shipment of grain from an elevator on a short line railroad might need to interchange to a Class I carrier for the long haul, then interchange again to a second short line at the destination end. That's two interchanges on a single move. Some shipments cross three or even four carriers, depending on geography.

How the Interchange Process Works, Step by Step

Interchange isn't just unhooking a car from one train and hooking it to another. It's a formalized process with specific steps that the railroads follow every time.

1

The Delivering Carrier Moves the Car to the Interchange Track

The railroad that currently has the car switches it from its train to a designated interchange track — a set of tracks where both railroads have access. Sometimes this is at a classification yard, sometimes at a dedicated interchange yard, and sometimes at a simple track connection between two railroads.

2

Car Inspection

The receiving railroad inspects the car before accepting it. They're checking for mechanical defects — bad brakes, damaged wheels, broken couplers, leaking valves on tank cars, load securement issues on flatcars. If the car fails inspection, it gets "bad ordered" and the delivering carrier has to fix the problem before the receiving road will take it. This is one of the most common sources of interchange delays.

3

Electronic Data Interchange (EDI)

The railroads exchange electronic records through the Railinc system, which is maintained by the Association of American Railroads (AAR). This includes the car's identity, its load status, origin, destination, routing, commodity code, weight, and hazmat classification if applicable. The EDI message effectively tells the receiving railroad "this car is now your responsibility."

4

Acceptance and Classification

Once the receiving railroad accepts the car, it moves into their operating system. The car gets classified — sorted into the right outbound train based on its destination. If the interchange point has a classification yard, this happens through the normal humping or flat-switching process. If it's a simpler connection, the car might wait for a scheduled local train to pick it up.

5

Departure on the Receiving Carrier's Train

The car joins an outbound train on the receiving railroad's network and continues toward its destination — or toward the next interchange, if there is one.

Each of these steps has to happen in sequence, and the car sits idle while it waits for each one. At a busy, well-run interchange point, the whole process might take under 24 hours. At a slower point, or during periods of congestion, it can stretch to several days.

Interchange Agreements and the AAR

Railroads don't just informally hand cars back and forth. The process is governed by interchange agreements — contracts between the carriers that spell out the terms of the exchange. These agreements cover everything from which tracks the cars are placed on to who pays for what and how quickly the receiving carrier has to pick up the car.

The foundation for most interchange agreements is the AAR's suite of rules and standards. The most important ones for shippers to know about:

  • Field Manual of the AAR Interchange Rules: This defines the mechanical standards a car has to meet to be accepted at interchange. If a car doesn't meet these standards, it can be rejected. The Field Manual is several hundred pages of detailed specifications for every component of a freight car.
  • Code of Car Hire Rules: This governs per diem and car hire — the daily charges that railroads pay each other for using cars that belong to another railroad or a private car owner. When your car is sitting at an interchange point, someone is paying per diem on it.
  • Circular OT-10: This establishes the standard route guides that define how a car should be routed from origin to destination across multiple carriers. It's the map that tells each railroad where to send the car next.

Beyond the AAR framework, individual railroads also negotiate bilateral agreements with each other for specific interchange points. These can include performance commitments (like maximum dwell time), switching fees, and dispute resolution processes. As a shipper, you rarely see these agreements directly, but their terms affect how quickly your freight moves through interchange.

Types of Interchange Points

Not all interchanges are the same. The type of interchange point your freight passes through has a significant impact on how long it sits there.

Major Classification Yard Interchanges

The highest-volume interchanges happen at large classification yards where two Class I railroads' networks overlap. These yards process thousands of cars per day and have dedicated interchange tracks, inspection teams, and electronic systems designed for speed. Examples include major junction points where eastern and western Class I carriers connect. These points handle the most traffic but also have the best infrastructure to keep cars moving.

Regional Junction Points

Mid-size interchanges where a Class I railroad connects with a regional railroad or where two regional carriers meet. These points handle moderate volumes and usually have dedicated staff, but they may only process interchange cars once or twice per day rather than continuously.

Short Line Connection Points

The most common type of interchange by count, but usually the smallest by volume. These are the points where a short line railroad connects to a Class I or regional carrier. Many short lines only interchange cars a few times per week, and the process may involve a single crew doing the switching, inspection, and paperwork. If your freight originates or terminates on a short line, this interchange is part of your supply chain.

Reciprocal Switching Points

In certain areas, regulators or agreements require railroads to provide "reciprocal switching" — where a shipper served by only one railroad can access a competing carrier through a short interchange move. The Surface Transportation Board (STB) can mandate reciprocal switching to promote competition. These interchange moves are typically short distances and relatively quick, but they add an extra handling step.

What Causes Interchange Delays

Interchange is where rail freight loses the most time, and the causes are varied. Knowing what to look for helps you diagnose problems when your shipment stalls.

Mechanical Failures (Bad Orders)

The most common cause. A receiving carrier inspects a car and finds a defective brake shoe, a cracked wheel, a coupler issue, or a problem with the car's draft gear. The car gets "bad ordered" and set aside until the defect is repaired. If the interchange point doesn't have a repair facility, the car might have to be moved to one — adding more delay. For older private cars, bad orders at interchange are a recurring headache.

Train Schedule Mismatches

The delivering carrier might drop a car at interchange at 2:00 AM, but the receiving carrier's next train through that point doesn't depart until 6:00 PM the following day. The car sits for 40 hours waiting for a ride. This is especially common at lower-volume interchange points where trains run on fixed schedules rather than as-needed.

Yard Congestion

During peak shipping seasons — fall grain harvest, construction material surges in spring, or holiday consumer goods pushes — classification yards can become congested. When a yard is backed up, interchange cars get lower priority than the receiving carrier's own traffic. Your car might clear inspection just fine but sit in the yard for days waiting for a slot on an outbound train.

Paperwork and Data Errors

If the EDI data doesn't match the physical car — wrong destination, incorrect weight, missing hazmat placard information — the receiving carrier will hold the car until the discrepancy is resolved. In the era of electronic data exchange, these errors are less common than they used to be, but they still happen, especially on moves involving multiple carriers where data gets re-entered at each handoff.

Weather and Force Majeure

Severe weather can shut down an interchange point entirely. Flooding, ice storms, and extreme heat (which can cause rail buckling and speed restrictions) all affect interchange operations. When a major interchange point goes down, the ripple effect can back up traffic across multiple railroads for days.

How Shippers Can Minimize Interchange Risk

You can't eliminate interchange from most rail moves, but you can take steps to reduce its impact on your supply chain.

Favor Single-Line Moves When Possible

If your origin and destination are both on the same Class I railroad's network, a single-line move avoids interchange entirely. This isn't always possible — geography dictates which railroads serve which points — but when you have a choice, single-line moves are more predictable. Our rail rate estimator shows the indicative line-haul cost on the lane you're considering — comparing single-line vs interchanged routing on the same origin–destination is a useful first step. Your rail logistics provider can then help identify whether a true single-line option exists for your lanes.

Minimize the Number of Interchanges

A move with one interchange is almost always better than one with two or three. Each interchange adds dwell time and another potential failure point. When routing options exist, the route with fewer interchanges is usually worth a modest rate premium for the consistency it buys you.

Know Your Interchange Points

Ask your carrier or logistics provider which interchange points your freight will pass through. Not all interchange points are equal — some are notoriously slow, others are well-run. If you know where the bottlenecks are, you can adjust your shipping schedule to buffer for them or request alternate routing. If neither end of your lane has direct rail access, our transload directory filters 800+ rail-served terminals by state and commodity — sometimes the right move is to shift origin or destination to a transload that sits on a friendlier interchange point.

Keep Your Equipment in Good Shape

If you're shipping in private or leased cars, mechanical failures at interchange are largely preventable with good maintenance. Bad orders are the single biggest cause of interchange delays for private equipment. A car that fails inspection doesn't just sit — it disrupts your whole supply chain timeline. Invest in regular inspections and preventive maintenance between trips.

Use Real-Time Tracking

The Class I railroads all offer shipment tracking through their websites and EDI systems. Monitor your cars as they approach interchange points. If a car has been sitting at interchange for longer than expected, contact your carrier or logistics provider immediately. Early intervention — a phone call to the right person at the receiving railroad — can sometimes shake a car loose faster than waiting.

Build Interchange Dwell Into Your Planning

The biggest mistake shippers make with interchange is not accounting for it in their supply chain planning. If your move involves two interchanges, build buffer time into your delivery expectations. Talk to your logistics provider about historical dwell times at the specific interchange points on your route. Planning for reality beats being surprised by it.

Per Diem, Car Hire, and the Cost Side of Interchange

Interchange doesn't just cost time — it costs money. Several charges are directly tied to the interchange process.

Per Diem Charges

When a railroad has possession of a car it doesn't own — whether it's another railroad's car or a privately owned/leased car — per diem charges accrue. Per diem is a daily rental fee, typically in the range of $30 to $80 per day depending on the car type and ownership. While the car sits at interchange waiting to be inspected, classified, and dispatched, per diem keeps ticking. For shippers who own or lease their equipment, this is a direct cost. For railroad-owned equipment, per diem gets factored into the freight rate.

Switching Charges

Some interchange moves involve switching charges — fees that one railroad charges for physically moving the car to or from the interchange track. These are most common at short line interchanges where the short line charges a per-car switching fee. Switching charges typically range from $200 to $500 per car, depending on the distance and complexity of the switch.

Division of Revenue

On multi-carrier moves, the total freight revenue gets divided among the participating railroads based on negotiated "divisions." Each railroad gets a share proportional to its contribution to the move (distance, difficulty, etc.). This revenue division is worked out between the carriers — shippers pay one through rate — but it matters because carriers with small revenue shares on a particular move have less financial incentive to prioritize those cars. This dynamic occasionally contributes to interchange delays.

Demurrage Implications

If interchange delays cause a car to arrive late at destination, the downstream demurrage implications can compound the cost. A car that was supposed to arrive on Tuesday but didn't clear interchange until Thursday might now conflict with your unloading schedule, leading to demurrage charges at the destination facility. The interchange delay itself doesn't trigger demurrage, but the cascading effect on your unloading window often does.

Bottom line: Every interchange adds cost — per diem, switching fees, and the indirect costs of unpredictability. When evaluating rail routes, factor these costs into your total landed cost, not just the line-haul rate. A slightly higher rate on a route with fewer interchanges often saves money overall. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a railroad interchange?

A railroad interchange is the physical and administrative process of transferring freight cars from one railroad carrier to another. It happens at designated interchange points where two or more railroads' tracks connect, and involves inspecting the car, exchanging paperwork, and handing off responsibility for the shipment.

How long does a railroad interchange take?

A single interchange can take anywhere from a few hours to several days. Efficient interchange points with high traffic volumes and strong operating agreements may process cars within hours. Less active points, or situations involving car inspection failures or paperwork issues, can add days to a shipment's transit time.

Who is responsible for freight during interchange?

Responsibility transfers from the delivering carrier to the receiving carrier once the car passes the interchange point and is accepted. The interchange rules published by the AAR define exactly when liability shifts. Until the receiving railroad formally accepts the car, the delivering carrier remains responsible for it.

Can shippers choose which interchange points their freight uses?

Shippers can influence routing through their bill of lading and by working with a rail logistics provider, but the railroads ultimately control operational routing. Specifying a preferred route or interchange point in your shipping instructions gives the carriers guidance, though they may deviate for operational reasons.

What is an interchange agreement between railroads?

An interchange agreement is a contract between two railroads that governs how they exchange freight cars at a shared interchange point. It covers car inspection standards, per diem charges, liability transfers, switching fees, and dwell time expectations. The AAR's Code of Car Hire Rules provides the baseline framework most agreements build on.
