Bulk Commodity Shipping by Rail: Aggregates, Steel, Fertilizer, and More

March 19, 2026 · 11 min read · Commodity Guide
Key fact: U.S. railroads move approximately 1.7 billion tons of freight annually, and bulk commodities — aggregates, grain, chemicals, fertilizer, steel, and coal — account for the vast majority of that volume. For shippers moving heavy, high-tonnage goods, rail remains the most cost-effective overland freight mode by a wide margin.

If you ship large quantities of raw materials or heavy goods, rail freight isn't just an option — it's probably the option that makes or breaks your margins. Bulk commodity shipping by rail moves everything from crushed stone to steel coils to potash, and the economics are hard to argue with: rail costs roughly one-quarter to one-half what trucking costs per ton-mile. But getting bulk commodities onto rail isn't as simple as loading a truck. Different commodities require different equipment, different service models, and different logistics planning. This guide covers the major bulk commodity categories, the equipment and service types that move them, and the practical decisions shippers face when building a rail freight program.

Why Bulk Commodities Move by Rail

The fundamental economics are straightforward: a single railcar carries the equivalent of three to five truckloads, depending on the commodity. A 100-car unit train replaces 300 to 500 truck trips. When you're shipping tens of thousands of tons per year — which is normal for aggregates, grain, fertilizer, and steel operations — that truck-to-rail conversion translates directly to lower freight costs, fewer road miles, and less operational complexity.

Rail's cost advantage is driven by physics. Steel wheels on steel rails produce far less friction than rubber tires on asphalt. One locomotive can pull tonnage that would require hundreds of individual truck engines. That efficiency compounds over distance: the longer the haul, the more rail saves you relative to trucking. For most bulk commodities, rail becomes cheaper than trucking at distances as short as 150 to 200 miles, and the gap widens considerably beyond that.

There's also a capacity argument. The trucking industry has dealt with chronic driver shortages for years. Bulk commodities are among the hardest trucking loads to staff — heavy, repetitive, often at remote loading sites. Rail doesn't have the same labor constraint per ton moved. For shippers who need to move consistent high volumes, rail provides capacity stability that trucking can't always match.

Major Bulk Commodity Categories

Bulk rail freight covers a wide range of products, but most volume falls into a handful of categories. Each has its own equipment requirements, seasonal patterns, and logistics considerations.

Aggregates and Construction Materials

Crushed stone, sand, gravel, and cement are among the heaviest commodities on the rail network. Aggregates ship by rail in open-top hoppers and gondolas, typically from quarries and mines to construction markets and ready-mix plants. Volumes track construction activity closely — when highway projects and commercial building ramp up, aggregate shipments surge. A single hopper car carries roughly 100 tons of crushed stone, replacing three to four truck trips.

Cement and fly ash move in covered hoppers with pneumatic discharge gates, since these materials can't tolerate moisture. The logistics often revolve around transloading: most job sites and batch plants aren't rail-served, so aggregates move by rail to a transload facility and then by truck for the final leg.

Steel and Metals

Steel is one of the densest commodities on the rail network, which makes it exceptionally efficient to move by rail. Steel and metals ship in gondolas, coil cars, and flatcars depending on the product form — coils, plate, structural shapes, pipe, or scrap. A loaded gondola of steel coils replaces four to five truckloads. Steel mills and scrap recyclers are almost universally rail-served because of the enormous tonnages involved. The U.S. rail network moves approximately 75 million tons of steel and metal products per year.

Scrap metal is a particularly high-volume rail commodity. Electric arc furnace (EAF) steel mills consume massive quantities of scrap, and rail is the dominant mode for long-distance scrap movements. Most shredders and scrap processors of any size have rail access for exactly this reason.

Fertilizer

Fertilizer moves by rail in covered hoppers (for dry products like potash, DAP, MAP, and urea) and tank cars (for anhydrous ammonia and liquid nitrogen solutions). The fertilizer supply chain is inherently long-haul — potash comes from mines, phosphate from processing plants, and nitrogen products from natural gas-based manufacturing facilities. These origins are often hundreds or thousands of miles from the farms and distributors that consume the product.

Fertilizer has some of the most pronounced seasonal demand swings of any rail commodity. Spring planting drives a surge in shipments from January through April, and fall application creates a secondary peak. Smart shippers build inventory during off-season months when car supply is abundant and rates are more favorable.

Grain and Agricultural Products

Grain ships by rail in covered hoppers, moving from country elevators and farm loading facilities to export terminals, flour mills, feed lots, and ethanol plants. Corn, wheat, soybeans, and sorghum are the primary rail grain commodities. Grain is the classic unit train commodity — shuttle trains of 75 to 110 covered hoppers cycle between origin and destination on a continuous loop during harvest season.

The grain supply chain is uniquely dependent on rail. Roughly 30% of all U.S. grain moves by rail at some point between farm and end-user, and for export grain, that figure is significantly higher. Country elevators that lack rail access face a permanent cost disadvantage.

Coal and Petroleum Coke

Coal has historically been the single largest commodity by tonnage on the U.S. rail network, though volumes have declined as power generation shifts toward natural gas and renewables. Coal moves in open-top gondolas and bottom-dump hopper cars, almost exclusively in unit trains of 100+ cars. The logistics are relatively simple — mine to power plant, often on dedicated routes — but the tonnages are enormous. Petroleum coke (petcoke), a refinery byproduct used in aluminum smelting and cement manufacturing, moves in similar equipment and follows similar logistics patterns.

Chemicals and Liquid Bulk

Chemical rail shipping covers a broad category: plastics (resin pellets in covered hoppers), liquid chemicals (tank cars), industrial gases, and various dry chemical compounds. Tank car shipments follow strict regulatory requirements — the car specification, pressure rating, and safety features must match the hazard class of the product. Chemical shippers often own or lease their own tank car fleets because the specialized equipment isn't always available from railroad pools.

Forest Products

Lumber, wood chips, pulp, and paper products round out the major bulk commodity categories. Lumber ships on flatcars and in boxcars. Wood chips — used in paper manufacturing — move in open-top gondolas or specially designed chip cars with extra-high sides to maximize volume. Pulp and paper travel in boxcars to protect them from weather. Like aggregates, forest product volumes track closely with construction and manufacturing activity.

Equipment Matching: The Right Car for the Right Commodity

One of the first decisions in any bulk rail shipping program is equipment selection. The wrong car type means loading problems, damage risk, regulatory violations, or wasted capacity. Here's the quick reference:

Commodity Primary Car Type Typical Capacity Key Feature
Crushed stone, sand, gravel Open-top hopper 100-110 tons Bottom-dump gates for fast unloading
Cement, fly ash Covered hopper (pneumatic) 95-100 tons Sealed from moisture, pneumatic discharge
Grain, dry fertilizer, potash Covered hopper 100-110 tons Gravity-discharge gates, weather protection
Steel coils, plate, structural Gondola / coil car 100-110 tons Open top for crane loading, cradles for coils
Scrap metal Open-top gondola 70-90 tons High sides, magnetic crane compatible
Anhydrous ammonia, liquid chemicals Tank car 80-90 tons (liquid) Pressure-rated, hazmat compliant
Coal, petcoke Open hopper / gondola 100-115 tons Rapid-discharge rotary dump compatible
Lumber, wood chips Flatcar / chip gondola 60-100 tons Volume-limited (light commodity)

Equipment sourcing is a real consideration. Railroads supply cars from their own fleets, but availability fluctuates with demand. During peak seasons, covered hoppers for grain and fertilizer get tight. Many high-volume shippers lease private car fleets to guarantee supply — it's a significant capital commitment, but it removes car availability as a variable in your supply chain.

Unit Trains vs. Manifest Service

How your freight moves through the rail network matters as much as what equipment it rides in. The two primary service models for bulk commodities are unit trains and manifest (carload) service.

Unit Trains

A unit train is a dedicated consist — typically 80 to 120 cars — carrying a single commodity from one origin to one destination. The train stays together from loading to unloading. It doesn't stop at classification yards for sorting. This makes unit trains faster, more predictable, and significantly cheaper per ton than manifest service.

Unit train rates can be 20-40% lower than manifest carload rates for the same commodity and lane. The railroad's operating cost is lower because there's no switching, no classification, no intermediate handling. The trade-off is volume commitment: you need enough tonnage to fill the train on a regular cycle. For grain, coal, and potash shippers with sufficient volume, unit trains are the default service model.

Manifest (Carload) Service

Manifest service handles individual cars or small groups of cars. The railroad collects cars from multiple shippers at a local yard, assembles them into road trains, sorts them at classification yards, and delivers them to destination yards for final placement. It's more flexible — you can ship one car or fifty — but it's slower and costs more per ton because of all the intermediate handling.

Most bulk shippers that aren't large enough for unit trains use manifest service. It's also the model for shippers who receive multiple commodity types at one facility, or who ship to multiple destinations that can't each justify a unit train.

The volume threshold: Unit trains typically require 8,000 to 12,000 tons per shipment (80-120 cars). If your annual volume exceeds 100,000 tons on a single lane, unit train service is almost certainly available and worth pursuing. Below that, manifest carload service — or multi-car rate programs — are the more practical option.

Transloading and Facility Access

One of the biggest practical barriers to bulk rail shipping is facility access. Not every shipper or receiver has a rail siding. Transloading solves this by providing a transfer point where freight moves between rail and truck.

For bulk commodities, transloading is extremely common. Aggregates get transloaded from hopper cars into trucks for delivery to construction sites. Fertilizer gets transloaded at distribution terminals for last-mile delivery to farms. Steel gets transloaded at service centers that break down railcar-quantity loads into truck-quantity deliveries to fabricators and end users.

A well-located transload facility turns rail access into a shared resource. You don't need to invest in your own siding, you don't need to own or maintain track, and you don't need to deal with railcar switching logistics at your facility. The transload operator handles all of that. Want to find one near you? Try our free transload facility directory — 800+ facilities, filterable by state and commodity. If your volume justifies it — generally 500+ carloads per year — a private rail spur or siding starts to make economic sense. The upfront cost is significant (typically $500,000 to $2 million+ depending on length, terrain, and track connection complexity), but it eliminates transloading fees and gives you direct rail access on your own schedule.

Cost Factors in Bulk Rail Shipping

Bulk rail rates aren't published like LTL carrier tariffs. Most bulk commodity movements are priced through contracts negotiated between the shipper (or their rail logistics provider) and the railroad. Several factors determine what you'll pay:

  • Distance: Longer hauls cost more in absolute terms but less per ton-mile. Rail's efficiency advantage over trucking grows with distance.
  • Volume commitment: Annual tonnage commitments get better rates. Unit train quantities get the best rates. Multi-year contracts can lock in favorable pricing.
  • Commodity type: Railroads price based on what the traffic will bear. High-value commodities (chemicals, finished steel) pay more per ton-mile than low-value bulk (sand, coal) because the shipper has more margin to absorb freight cost.
  • Car supply: If you use railroad-supplied cars, availability affects pricing. Leasing your own fleet gives rate leverage because the railroad's car investment is zero.
  • Origin/destination pairs: Routes with balanced traffic (loaded moves in both directions) are cheaper than routes where cars return empty. Some lanes are simply more competitive than others because multiple railroads serve the corridor.
  • Fuel surcharges: Railroads pass through fuel cost fluctuations via surcharges indexed to diesel prices. These are separate from the base rate and can add 10-20% to total cost depending on fuel markets.
  • Accessorial charges: Demurrage (charges for holding cars beyond free time), switching fees, diversion charges, and other accessorials add up if not managed carefully.
Rule of thumb: Bulk rail rates typically fall between $0.02 and $0.05 per ton-mile, compared to $0.10 to $0.20 per ton-mile for trucking. On a 500-mile haul of 100 tons, that's roughly $1,000-$2,500 by rail versus $5,000-$10,000 by truck.

Seasonal Demand Patterns

Bulk commodity rail shipping is heavily seasonal, and understanding the cycles is critical for controlling costs and securing car supply.

Spring (March-May): Fertilizer demand peaks as planting season begins. Construction activity ramps up, driving aggregate shipments higher. Covered hopper and open-top hopper availability tightens. This is the most competitive period for equipment.

Summer (June-August): Construction and aggregate volumes peak. Fertilizer demand drops post-planting. Grain shipments are relatively low between old-crop depletion and new-crop harvest. Overall, equipment supply loosens slightly as fertilizer cars free up.

Fall (September-November): Grain harvest drives a massive surge in covered hopper demand. Fall fertilizer application creates a secondary peak. Construction winds down in northern markets but continues in temperate zones. This is the tightest period for covered hoppers — plan ahead.

Winter (December-February): Most commodity volumes decline. Equipment is generally available. This is the best time to negotiate rates, secure car commitments, and build inventory for spring. Shippers who can take delivery during winter months often get meaningful rate concessions.

The shippers who manage seasonality well — pre-positioning inventory, locking in car supply before peak seasons, negotiating multi-season contracts — consistently get better rates and more reliable service than those who try to secure equipment at the last minute during peak demand.

Building a Bulk Rail Program

If you're shipping bulk commodities by truck today and considering a shift to rail — or if you're already shipping some volume by rail and want to optimize — here's the practical sequence:

1

Assess your volume and lanes

Map your annual tonnage by origin-destination pair. Identify lanes where you're moving 5,000+ tons per year over 200+ miles — those are your rail candidates. Calculate what you're spending on trucking for those lanes today.

2

Identify rail access points

Determine whether your origin and destination facilities are rail-served. If not, locate nearby transload facilities. Your rail logistics provider or the serving railroad's industrial development team can help identify options.

3

Match equipment to your commodity

Determine the correct car type for your product (see the equipment table above). Decide whether to use railroad-supplied equipment, lease private cars, or work with a car-supply intermediary. For volumes under 500 carloads per year, railroad-supplied equipment is usually the starting point.

4

Negotiate rates and service

Work with the railroad directly or through a rail logistics provider to negotiate rates. Bring your volume data, lane information, and competitive truck rates to the table. Volume commitments, multi-year terms, and off-peak flexibility all give you negotiating leverage.

5

Set up operations

Establish loading and unloading procedures, car ordering processes, and tracking systems. Define who manages demurrage — unloading cars quickly is one of the biggest operational cost levers in bulk rail. Build relationships with the local railroad operations team.

6

Optimize over time

Once you're moving volume, look for optimization opportunities: consolidating lanes for unit train eligibility, shifting shipment timing to off-peak windows, negotiating car supply commitments, and reducing demurrage through faster loading and unloading cycles.

The first rail shipment is always the hardest. There's paperwork, there's a learning curve on car ordering and tracking, and the timelines are longer than trucking. But once the program is established, the economics speak for themselves — and most shippers who convert volume to rail never go back.

Working with a Rail Logistics Provider

Most bulk shippers don't interact with Class I railroads directly for every operational detail. A rail logistics provider handles the coordination layer — rate negotiation, car supply management, tracking, demurrage monitoring, and transload coordination. This is especially valuable for shippers new to rail or those shipping across multiple railroad networks where interchange coordination adds complexity.

The right partner knows which railroads serve which facilities, how to structure rate deals for maximum leverage, where equipment supply is tight versus available, and how to route freight efficiently when the origin and destination are served by different carriers. For bulk commodities, where margins are tight and logistics complexity is real, that expertise translates directly to lower costs and fewer problems.

Frequently Asked Questions

What commodities are best suited for bulk rail shipping?

Commodities that are heavy, high-volume, and not time-sensitive perform best on rail. The top categories include aggregates (crushed stone, sand, gravel), grain, fertilizer, steel and metals, coal, chemicals, and forest products. The common thread is high tonnage that would require dozens of trucks per shipment.

How much does bulk commodity rail shipping cost per ton?

Bulk rail rates typically range from $0.02 to $0.05 per ton-mile, compared to $0.10 to $0.20 for trucking. Actual costs depend on commodity type, distance, volume commitment, and whether you ship via unit train or manifest service.

What is the difference between a unit train and manifest service?

A unit train is a dedicated consist of 80-120 cars carrying one commodity point-to-point without stopping at classification yards. Manifest service mixes cars from multiple shippers and routes them through yards for sorting. Unit trains are faster and 20-40% cheaper per ton, but require volume to fill the full train.

Do I need rail access at my facility to ship bulk commodities?

No. Transloading facilities provide transfer points where freight moves between rail cars and trucks. Many bulk shippers operate long-term via transload without ever building their own rail siding. If volume justifies it (500+ carloads/year), a private spur may make economic sense.

How do seasonal patterns affect bulk rail shipping costs?

Bulk rail shipping is heavily seasonal. Spring and fall see the tightest equipment supply (fertilizer and grain compete for covered hoppers). Winter is generally the best time to negotiate rates and build inventory. Shippers who plan around these cycles consistently get better rates and more reliable car supply.
