The Railroad Network

Class I Railroads: The Big Six

The North American freight railroad network is dominated by six Class I railroads — carriers with annual revenue over $900 million. These are the long-haul backbone of rail freight. Between them, they operate roughly 140,000 miles of track and handle about 70% of all U.S. rail freight revenue.

Western U.S.

BNSF Railway

  • Owned by Berkshire Hathaway
  • 32,500 route miles
  • Strong in multimodal, coal, grain
  • Dominates West Coast ports
Western U.S.

Union Pacific (UP)

  • Largest U.S. railroad by revenue
  • 32,200 route miles
  • Major chemical & industrial shipper
  • Gulf Coast & West Coast reach
Eastern U.S.

CSX Transportation

  • 21,000 route miles
  • Strong in coal, aggregates, autos
  • East Coast & Southeast
  • Major multimodal network
Eastern U.S.

Norfolk Southern (NS)

  • 19,500 route miles
  • Coal, multimodal, metals
  • East Coast & Midwest reach
  • Major auto carrier
Canada + U.S.

Canadian National (CN)

  • Only railroad spanning Canada coast-to-coast
  • Reaches Gulf of Mexico
  • Strong in grain & petroleum
  • ~20,000 route miles
Canada + U.S.

CPKC (CP Kansas City)

  • Merged CP + KCS in 2023
  • First single-line Canada–Mexico railroad
  • ~20,000 route miles
  • Strong in cross-border freight
Why this matters to shippers Most long-haul rail shipments touch at least one Class I railroad. If your freight moves 500+ miles by rail, you're almost certainly dealing with one of these carriers. Knowing their territories helps you understand routing options and potential bottlenecks.

Regional & Short Line Railroads

Below the Class I carriers are roughly 600 regional and short line railroads. These smaller carriers are the "first mile / last mile" of rail freight — they connect shippers' facilities to the Class I network.

Regional Railroads

  • Revenue $40M–$900M annually
  • Typically 400–1,500 route miles
  • Serve specific geographic corridors
  • Often former Class I branch lines

Short Line Railroads

  • Revenue under $40M annually
  • Typically under 400 route miles
  • First/last mile service to shippers
  • Handle switching, local delivery
The interchange When a short line picks up cars from a shipper's facility and delivers them to a Class I railroad (or vice versa), that handoff is called an interchange. Understanding where interchanges happen is critical — it's where delays often occur and where coordination matters most.

Types of Rail Service

Not all rail freight moves the same way. There are three main service types, and the economics are very different for each.

🚃

Carload (Manifest)

Individual cars from different shippers grouped into trains at classification yards. Flexible but slower — cars get switched between trains. 1–15 cars per shipper.

🚂

Unit Train

A full train (80–120 cars) of one commodity from one origin to one destination. No switching, no classification. Fastest, cheapest per-ton rail service. Coal, grain, crude oil.

📦

Multimodal

Containers or trailers that ride on rail flatcars for the long haul, then transfer to trucks for local delivery. Competes directly with over-the-road trucking.

Unit train economics A unit train of 110 cars carrying coal moves about 12,000 tons in a single trip — the equivalent of roughly 400 truckloads. The railroad gives significant rate discounts for unit trains because they don't need classification yard handling and they keep the network fluid. If you're shipping enough volume, unit trains are the most cost-effective way to move bulk commodities.

Rail Car Types

Different commodities require different cars. Knowing which car type moves which product is fundamental to rail logistics.

Covered Hopper

  • Enclosed top, gravity-discharge bottom
  • Hauls: grain, cement, sand, plastic pellets, flour
  • Protects cargo from weather
  • Most common car for dry bulk

Open-Top Hopper

  • No roof — loaded from above
  • Hauls: coal, aggregates, ballast, ore
  • Gravity discharge through bottom gates
  • Commodity doesn't need weather protection

Gondola

  • Open-top, flat-bottom or drop-bottom
  • Hauls: scrap metal, steel coils, lumber, aggregates
  • Loaded by crane, conveyor, or dump truck
  • Versatile — handles irregular-shaped loads

Tank Car

  • Cylindrical — liquid and gas transport
  • Hauls: chemicals, petroleum, ethanol, food-grade oils
  • DOT-regulated (especially hazmat)
  • Pressurized or non-pressurized variants

Boxcar

  • Fully enclosed with sliding side doors
  • Hauls: paper, packaged goods, auto parts, canned goods
  • Protects cargo from weather and theft
  • Being displaced by multimodal containers

Flatcar / Centerbeam

  • Flat deck — no sides or roof
  • Hauls: lumber, steel beams, machinery, military equipment
  • Centerbeam variant: vertical divider for lumber/drywall
  • TOFC/COFC: trailers/containers ride on flatcars
Private vs. railroad-owned cars Some cars are owned by the railroad, but many are privately owned — by shippers, leasing companies, or car-supply firms. If you ship high volumes regularly, leasing your own cars can save significantly vs. paying railroad-supplied car rates. The trade-off is the capital commitment and maintenance responsibility.

Key Rail Terminology

Rail has its own language. Here are the terms you'll encounter constantly when working with railroads.

Waybill

The railroad's shipping document. Contains origin, destination, commodity, car number, routing, and rate. Every car that moves has a waybill.

Bill of Lading (BOL)

The shipper's contract with the carrier. Specifies what's being shipped, quantity, origin/destination. Legal document of ownership.

Demurrage

Penalty charges when you keep a railroad's car past the free time allowed for loading/unloading. Typically $75–$200/car/day. Adds up fast.

Per Diem

Daily rental fee charged for using a privately-owned car. Owed by the railroad holding the car to the car's owner. Usually $30–$80/day.

Interchange

The point where one railroad hands off cars to another railroad. Where short lines meet Class I carriers, or where two Class I territories meet.

Switching / Spotting

Switching = moving cars within a yard or between tracks. Spotting = placing a car at a specific location (loading dock, siding) for the shipper.

Siding / Spur

A short section of track branching off the main line, usually at a shipper's facility. Where cars are spotted for loading/unloading.

Classification Yard

A large rail yard where incoming trains are broken apart and individual cars are sorted and reassembled into new outbound trains based on destination.

  1. Car spotted at shipper's facility — Empty car placed on the shipper's siding or at a transload facility
  2. Shipper loads the car — Commodity loaded, car sealed, paperwork (waybill/BOL) submitted
  3. Local switch crew picks up — Car moved to the origin classification yard
  4. Classification & line haul — Car sorted into an outbound train, travels the main line. May pass through multiple classification yards if the route involves interchange between railroads
  5. Destination yard — Car arrives and is sorted for local delivery
  6. Local delivery — Switch crew spots the car at the receiver's facility for unloading
Where the time goes On a 1,500-mile carload move, the actual line-haul running time might be 3 days. But the total transit time is often 7–10 days because of the time spent sitting in classification yards waiting to be sorted into the next train. Unit trains skip this entirely — which is why they're so much faster.

Rail vs. Truck: When Each Wins

Rail and trucking aren't enemies — they serve different niches. The key is knowing when each mode makes economic sense.

Rail Wins When

  • Distance is 500+ miles
  • Shipment is heavy/bulky (bulk commodities)
  • Volume is high and consistent
  • Speed is not the top priority
  • You're shipping 100+ tons per move
  • Origin and destination are rail-served

Truck Wins When

  • Distance is under 500 miles
  • Shipment is time-sensitive
  • Destination isn't rail-served
  • Volume is small or irregular
  • Door-to-door flexibility needed
  • Frequent, small deliveries

Rail Cost Advantage

~3-4x more fuel efficient than truck

One train can move the tonnage of 300+ trucks

Cost per ton-mile: rail ~$0.02–0.04 vs truck ~$0.08–0.15

Truck Speed Advantage

Door-to-door, typically 2-3x faster

No classification yards, no interchange delays

Flexible pickup and delivery scheduling

The real decision For bulk commodities moving long distances, rail isn't just cheaper — it's the only practical option. You can't economically move 12,000 tons of coal by truck. For manufactured goods and consumer products, it's usually a cost-vs-speed calculation. Multimodal tries to give you the best of both: rail economics for the long haul, truck flexibility for the first/last mile.

What Moves by Rail

Rail carries about 40% of all U.S. freight tonnage. Here are the major commodity groups.

Energy

Coal & Petroleum

  • Coal: still ~20% of rail revenue
  • Crude oil: unit trains to refineries
  • Ethanol: major Midwest product
  • Natural gas liquids
Agriculture

Grain & Farm Products

  • Wheat, corn, soybeans
  • Export terminals (Gulf, PNW)
  • Feed mills & ethanol plants
  • Fertilizers (potash, urea)
Industrial

Chemicals & Materials

  • Industrial chemicals (chlorine, caustic soda)
  • Aggregates (sand, gravel, crushed stone)
  • Steel, metals, lumber
  • Cement & construction materials
Rail's sweet spot The pattern is clear: heavy, bulky, non-time-sensitive commodities that move in large quantities between fixed points. If you're shipping 10 containers of electronics from LA to Chicago, multimodal is your play. If you're shipping 100 cars of grain from Kansas to the Gulf, that's a unit train.

The Role of a Rail Logistics Provider

Most shippers don't work directly with railroads. They work with a rail logistics provider who handles the complexity. Here's what that relationship looks like.

🏭

The Shipper

Needs to move commodity from A to B. May not have rail expertise, railroad relationships, or time to manage rail operations.

🤝

Rail Logistics Provider

Coordinates between shipper and railroads. Handles routing, rate negotiation, car supply, tracking, and problem resolution.

🚂

The Railroad

Owns the track and equipment. Moves the freight. Sets rates and service levels. Massive operations — hard to navigate alone.

A rail logistics provider adds value by:

  • Rate negotiation — Leveraging volume and relationships to get competitive pricing
  • Routing optimization — Choosing the best route considering transit time, interchanges, and reliability
  • Car supply management — Ensuring empty cars are available when the shipper needs them
  • Tracking & visibility — Monitoring shipments and proactively handling exceptions
  • Problem resolution — When cars go missing, get misrouted, or sit in yards too long
  • Transload coordination — Arranging truck-to-rail or rail-to-truck transfers when needed
Why it matters Railroads are built to move trains, not to hold your hand. Their customer service infrastructure is designed for high-volume, repeat shippers. A rail logistics provider bridges that gap — especially for mid-size shippers who need rail economics but don't ship enough to command railroad attention on their own.

Key Takeaways

  1. Six Class I railroads handle ~70% of U.S. rail freight revenue. Know their territories.
  2. Short lines are the first/last mile — they connect your facility to the Class I network at interchange points.
  3. Three service types: carload (flexible, slower), unit train (cheapest per-ton, bulk only), multimodal (competes with trucking).
  4. Six main car types — each designed for specific commodities. Covered hoppers, open hoppers, gondolas, tank cars, boxcars, and flatcars.
  5. Rail shipments move through classification yards — that's where most transit time is consumed, not on the main line.
  6. Rail beats trucking on cost for heavy, bulk freight over 500+ miles. Truck wins on speed and flexibility for shorter distances.
  7. Rail carries 40% of U.S. freight tonnage — mostly coal, grain, chemicals, aggregates, petroleum, and multimodal containers.
  8. A rail logistics provider bridges the gap between shippers and railroads — handling coordination, rates, car supply, and problem resolution.

Glossary

Class I Railroad

A freight railroad with annual revenue exceeding $900M. Six operate in North America: BNSF, UP, CSX, NS, CN, and CPKC.

Unit Train

A dedicated train (80–120 cars) carrying a single commodity from one origin to one destination. No classification yard stops. Most cost-effective rail service.

Multimodal

Freight that moves in containers or trailers using multiple transportation modes (typically rail + truck). Container rides a flatcar for the long haul, then transfers to a truck chassis.

Classification Yard

A large rail yard where trains are disassembled and individual cars are sorted by destination, then reassembled into new outbound trains.

Demurrage

Charges assessed when a railroad-owned car is held by a shipper/receiver beyond the allotted free time for loading or unloading.

Interchange

The physical location and process where one railroad hands off cars to another railroad. Where short lines connect to Class I networks.

Covered Hopper

An enclosed rail car with gravity-discharge bottom gates. Used for dry bulk commodities that need weather protection: grain, cement, sand, plastic pellets.

Waybill

The railroad's primary shipping document for each car. Contains origin, destination, commodity, routing instructions, and rate information.
