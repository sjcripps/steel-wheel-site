Rail-Served Port Warehousing & Breakbulk Terminals

24 U.S. port terminal complexes where on-dock, rail-served warehouse space is published by the port authority itself — covered storage at the berth with track to (and in several cases inside) the building, and the serving railroad named for each. As far as we know this is the only assembled national directory of rail-served port warehousing with the serving railroads listed.

Why it matters for breakbulk: steel, forest products, project cargo and bagged goods rarely move vessel-to-railcar on the same day. On-dock rail-served warehousing lets you store between the vessel and the railcar and make one drayage-free handoff inside the terminal — no truck leg to an off-port warehouse, no second lift at a separate rail yard. For a shipper deciding where to move breakbulk through, the questions are which ports have covered rail-served storage and which railroads can pick it up. Both answers are on this page.

How port terminal railways work

Most major breakbulk ports are switched by a port terminal railway — a short switching railroad, usually port-owned or jointly owned by the Class I's, that runs the trackage on the docks and interchanges with the main-line carriers. Mobile has the Terminal Railway TASD, New Orleans the New Orleans Public Belt (NOPB), Houston the Port Terminal Railroad Association (PTRA). The practical effect: one terminal railway connecting multiple Class I's means your cargo is not captive to a single carrier — the port switch pulls the cars off the dock and hands them to whichever main-line railroad wins your lane. When you compare ports below, look at how many Class I's the terminal railway reaches; it is one of the biggest levers on rate competition out of a port.

Frequently asked questions

What is breakbulk cargo?

Breakbulk is cargo that moves as individual pieces rather than in a shipping container or loose in a bulk hold — steel coils and plate, lumber and paper rolls, project cargo, machinery, wind components, bagged goods on pallets. Each piece is lifted, stowed and stored individually, which is why breakbulk terminals are built around transit sheds, laydown yards and heavy-lift cranes rather than container stacks.

What does “on-dock rail” mean at a port warehouse?

On-dock rail means track runs to the warehouse or berth itself, so cargo transfers between vessel, shed and railcar inside the terminal — no truck drayage leg between the port and a separate rail yard. Some facilities in this directory go further and run spurs inside the building (Pascagoula's transit sheds, PhilaPort's forest-products warehouse), so railcars load and unload under roof.

Who operates port warehouses?

It varies. Some warehouses are owned and operated by the port authority itself; many are run by terminal operators or stevedores under lease or concession (Logistec at Cleveland, Enstructure at Wilmington); others are leased to private 3PLs. The port authority is usually the right first call — they publish the facility specs cited on this page and can direct you to the operator handling your commodity.

How this directory is compiled. Every facility on this page is a port authority (or port terminal operator) whose own published materials state rail-served warehouse or transit-shed space — square footage, serving railroad and cargo focus are taken from those materials, not inferred from a map. This list is not exhaustive. A port missing from it has not been ruled out; it means we have not yet confirmed published rail-served warehouse specs for it. Steel Wheel Logistics does not own or operate these facilities and a listing does not imply a commercial relationship.
