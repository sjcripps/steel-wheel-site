Why We Built a Free Rail Logistics Course

Most rail logistics training is locked behind one of three doors: a carrier customer-academy program (BNSF, NS, UP, CSX, CN, CPKC each run their own), an industry conference circuit (NEARS, CSCMP, RailTrends), or a paid certification. Each is excellent in its slot. None are useful when a new ops hire starts on a Tuesday and you need to ramp them on demurrage rules before Thursday's call with the railroad.

Steel Wheel works with shippers every week who fit that gap. Procurement teams scoping a first-time rail bid. Plant managers inheriting a rail program from a retiring colleague. Finance directors who need to read a rail freight invoice without flagging every line for review. We built the course as an on-demand reference for those teams. Free, no upsell, no certification body to lobby — just the practitioner-level knowledge needed to run a rail program well.

The 10 Modules at a Glance

Each module is a standalone unit. You can take them in order or jump straight to the topic you need. Every module includes narrated video, written study guide, key terms, and review questions.

Module 1: Rail Freight Fundamentals

How a freight railroad moves a carload. Class I vs regional vs short line. The carload-to-classification-yard-to-interchange-to-destination flow that drives every other decision in this course.

Module 2: Working with Railroads

Account setup, customer service organizations, the bill-of-lading and waybill flow, EDI 404/214/417 messaging, and how to escalate a service problem without burning the relationship.

Module 3: Railroad Pricing

How rail rates are actually built. Tariff vs contract, fuel surcharge tariffs (BNSF 6100-B, UP 6004, CSX 8662, NS, CN 7403, CPKC 9700), rate negotiation framework, and the levers shippers genuinely have. Pairs with the rail-freight-rates pricing guide.

Module 4: Transloading

When transloading makes sense, site selection, economics, and the operational gotchas. Pairs with our transload terminal directory and the what-is-transloading explainer.

Module 5: Demurrage

Free time rules, accessorial structure, common dispute scenarios, and how to set up your operation so demurrage stays small. Pairs with the demurrage calculator and the demurrage fees guide.

Module 6: Safety, Compliance, and Regulations

FRA rules every shipper should know, hazmat placarding and documentation, AAR mechanical standards for railcars, and the compliance surface area that actually creates risk for non-operating shippers.

Module 7: Bulk Commodity Deep Dives

Grain, chemicals and hazmat, aggregates, coal, ethanol, frac sand, and forest products. Equipment, lane patterns, seasonal dynamics, and pricing benchmarks for each. The longest module in the course — treat it as a reference.

Module 8: Surviving PSR

Precision Scheduled Railroading: what it changed for shippers, how to read a railroad's PSR-driven service plan, and how to position your freight for the new operating model. Honest assessment, not promotional material.

Module 9: Technology and the Future of Rail

Visibility and tracking platforms, autonomous track inspection, hydrogen and battery locomotive trials, and what's actually mature vs hype. Built to help you cut through vendor pitch decks.

Module 10: Building Your Rail Strategy

The capstone module. Rail readiness assessment, modal conversion math, the RFP process for rail service, fleet management, and a 12-month implementation plan. Final assignment: build your own 12-month rail strategy for a real or hypothetical lane.

Who This Course Is For

Five audiences, all of whom we've watched walk into rail with no map:

  • Shippers evaluating rail for the first time. If your freight has never touched a railcar and you're trying to figure out whether it should, Modules 1, 4, and 10 will give you the math and framework to answer the question honestly.
  • Ops managers who inherited a rail program. Someone retired, you're now responsible for 12 inbound carloads of polymer per week, and you need to know what you're managing. Modules 2, 3, 5, and 6 are your survival kit.
  • Supply chain analysts running cost-comparison work. If your job involves rail vs truck cost analysis or modal-shift decisions, Modules 3, 7, and 10 give you the unit economics to model rail credibly.
  • Finance teams scoping a modal-shift business case. Module 10's modal conversion math is built specifically for the kind of business case CFOs and finance directors approve. Pair it with our CFO-ready rail business case post.
  • 3PL and freight broker reps who want to talk rail credibly. If you sell trucking and a customer asks about rail, you can either say "we don't do that" or you can have an informed conversation. Modules 1 through 5 are the floor.

Three Ways to Use It

Different teams use the course differently. The three patterns we see most often:

1. Linear ramp for new hires. Module 1 to Module 10 in order, ~30-45 minutes per module, paired with a written assignment from the team's manager. Companies onboarding into rail logistics roles often use this as a structured ramp during the first two weeks.

2. Reference-mode lookup. Bookmark the course, dip into the relevant module the day a topic hits a real shipment. Demurrage call coming up? Module 5. Transload economics question from a customer? Module 4. Fuel surcharge spike on the May invoice? Module 3.

3. Internal training cohort. A cross-functional team (procurement + ops + finance) takes the course together over four weeks, meets weekly to discuss the review questions, and builds the Module 10 final assignment as a real rail strategy document. The most ambitious use, and the one with the highest payoff — we've seen this pattern produce live RFPs.

Format and Time Commitment

Each module: ~30-45 minutes of narrated video, plus a written study guide that covers the same material in text form (skim-able in 10-15 minutes). Total course length is roughly 8 hours of video. Realistic time-to-completion if you treat it as a ramp: two to three weeks at one module per session. As reference material: indefinite — come back when the topic hits.

The format intentionally pairs video with text. Video is fine for the first pass, but when you're scoping a real shipment at 4:30 on a Wednesday afternoon, you don't want to scrub through a 40-minute video to find the demurrage formula. The written study guides exist for that.

How long does the course take?

About 8 hours total if you watch every module end to end, but the design assumes you'll use it as a reference. Is this a certification course?

No formal certification — there's no industry-recognized rail logistics certification body for non-operating personnel. What you get is a final assignment in Module 10 (a 12-month rail strategy for a hypothetical or real lane) and a structured set of review questions for each module. Companies that have used the course for internal onboarding pair it with a written assignment that the new hire submits to a manager. Works well as a structured ramp.

Do I need an existing rail program to get value from the course?

No. The earliest modules are explicitly built for shippers who have never moved a railcar — Module 10 includes a rail readiness assessment that walks you through whether your freight, volume, and lane geography even support rail. If the answer is no, the course tells you that. We'd rather you know the truth before you sink time into an RFP that won't pencil out.

How does this course compare to paid rail freight training?

Paid rail training in the U.S. usually comes from carrier-led customer programs (BNSF Customer Academy, Norfolk Southern Premier Customer Program, etc.) or industry associations (NEARS conferences, CSCMP rail tracks). Those are excellent if you can attend them. The Steel Wheel course is built to fill the gap for the rest of the year — when you need to ramp a new hire on a Tuesday, brief a CFO on Wednesday, or refresh on demurrage rules before a Thursday call. Free, on-demand, and written for the practitioner.

Does the course teach you how to negotiate rail freight rates?

Module 3 (Railroad Pricing) covers the structural side: how Class I tariffs are built, how fuel surcharges work, how contract rates differ from public tariffs, and what levers shippers actually have during negotiation (volume commitments, lane bundling, equipment supply, transit performance bonuses). The course does not promise that any specific tactic will lower your rate — railroad pricing is governed by commercial reality, contract structure, and market conditions. What you get is the framework to negotiate with eyes open.
