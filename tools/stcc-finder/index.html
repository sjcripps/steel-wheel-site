How STCC codes work

The Standard Transportation Commodity Code is a 7-digit number that tells the railroad exactly what's in the car. It's hierarchical: the first 2 digits are the broad industry group (01 = farm products, 33 = primary metals, 48 = hazardous waste), digits 3–5 narrow to the commodity family, and digits 6–7 pin down the exact article. Example: 01 farm products → 0113 grain → 01132 corn → a 7-digit corn code on the waybill.

Who maintains it

STCC is maintained by Railinc on behalf of the Association of American Railroads. The full 7-digit directory is a licensed publication, so this tool shows the public 2–5 digit hierarchy from the STB Waybill Reference Guide plus 7-digit codes the railroads themselves publish: CSX, UP and NS commodity lists and CN's List of Bulk STCCs under Tariffs 7402/7403, which alone contributes over 1,600 codes across grain, food, fertilizer, chemicals, coal and hazardous materials. Where a 5-digit family shows a carload and tonnage figure, it comes from the STB Public Use Carload Waybill Sample for 2024, expanded to the full population by the sample's own factor, so you can see how much of a commodity actually moves by rail before you commit to a code.

Why hazmat is different

Codes in the 48-series (hazardous waste) and 49-series (hazardous materials) aren't picked by commodity name — they're keyed to your 49 CFR shipping description. The proper shipping name, hazard class, and packing group on your hazmat paperwork determine the code, and the two must match. If your material is regulated, start from the 49 CFR description, not from a keyword search. That's also why the STB's public list translates 48/49-series traffic back to product codes: one product can carry a different code the moment it becomes a waste.

Why the code matters commercially

Rates, rules, and even whether a railroad will handle the shipment all hang off the STCC. The same physical material can rate very differently — prime steel coil vs. steel scrap, virgin glycol vs. spent antifreeze — so classifying correctly is money, not paperwork.
