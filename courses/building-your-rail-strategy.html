Part A

Rail Readiness Assessment

Is rail right for your freight? Let's do the math

Is Rail Right for Your Freight?

  • Rail Works Best When
  • Volume: 25+ carloads per month (or growing toward it)
  • Distance: 500+ miles (rail wins on long haul)
  • Commodity: bulk, heavy, non-time-sensitive
  • Consistency: regular, predictable shipments
  • You have or can access a rail siding/transload
  • Cost savings of 20-40% vs. truck are typical
  • Rail May Not Fit When
  • Volume: fewer than 10 loads per month
  • Distance: under 200 miles (truck usually wins)
  • Time-sensitive: next-day or just-in-time delivery
  • Small quantities: LTL-equivalent loads
  • No rail access and transload adds too much cost
  • Commodity requires temperature control (limited options)

The Modal Conversion Decision: Running the Numbers

  • STEP 1: Current truck cost per ton: (total freight spend / total tons shipped)
  • STEP 2: Estimated rail cost per ton: (rail rate + transload fee if applicable + drayage)
  • STEP 3: Savings per ton: truck cost - rail cost = your rail advantage
  • STEP 4: Annual savings: savings per ton x annual tonnage = gross savings
  • STEP 5: Subtract startup costs: siding construction, lease deposits, equipment, training
  • STEP 6: Payback period: startup costs / annual savings = months to break even
  • TYPICAL RESULT: 20-40% cost reduction, 6-18 month payback on infrastructure
  • IMPORTANT: Include all costs — demurrage risk, inventory changes, transit time impact

Modal Conversion Example: 50,000 Tons/Year

  • Current: Truck. $215/ton. 100% truck, 600-mile haul. 50,000 tons/year
  • Total annual cost: $10.75M
  • Simple, reliable, but expensive
  • Proposed: Rail. $130/ton
  • Rail rate + transload + drayage
  • Same 50,000 tons/year
  • Total annual cost: $6.50M. 39% cost reduction
  • Net Savings. $4.25M/yr
  • Startup costs: ~$500K (siding, equipment)
  • Payback period: ~1.4 months. 5-year savings: $20M+
  • Plus ESG benefits for reporting
Part B

Implementation & Relationships

How to get rail service started and build productive railroad partnerships

Building Railroad Relationships

  • Your railroad account representative is your primary contact — get to know them personally
  • Understand THEIR priorities: car velocity (turns), revenue per unit, network fluidity
  • Be a good customer: fast car turns, accurate orders, reliable volume = better service and rates
  • Attend railroad shipper conferences (BNSF, UP, CSX, NS all host annual events)
  • Join shipper advocacy groups: NITL (National Industrial Transportation League), MARS, NEARS
  • If on a short line: build a direct relationship with their commercial team — they're more flexible
  • Interline shipments: your origin railroad handles the relationship, but know all carriers on your route
  • Never burn a bridge — the rail world is small and account reps talk to each other

RFP Process for Rail Service

  • Define your requirements: commodity, volume (annual & peak monthly), origin, destination, car type
  • Contact all railroads serving your origin (or nearest transload facility)
  • Request proposals that include: rate per car, fuel surcharge method, minimum volume, contract term
  • Compare apples to apples: base rate + fuel surcharge + accessorials = total delivered cost
  • Negotiate key terms: demurrage credits/caps, performance guarantees, rate escalation formula
  • Multi-year contracts (3-5 years) typically get better rates than spot or annual
  • Volume commitments cut both ways: lower rates but you're on the hook for minimum volume
  • Get legal review before signing — railroad contracts heavily favor the railroad

Railcar Fleet Management

  • Fleet Sizing
  • Calculate: monthly volume / trips per car per month
  • Add 10-15% buffer for maintenance and dwell
  • Example: 100 cars/month at 3 trips = 33-38 car fleet
  • Start conservative — add cars as volume proves out
  • Lease terms: 1-7 years (longer = cheaper per month)
  • Review fleet utilization quarterly. ⚙️
  • Daily Operations
  • Track every car: location, status, free time remaining
  • Daily car report to operations manager
  • Pre-stage loading/unloading for arriving cars
  • Release empties same day as unloading
  • Bad-order inspection before release
  • Demurrage coordinator role is mandatory. 🔧
  • Maintenance & Returns
  • Periodic inspection per AAR standards
  • Track maintenance due dates by car number
  • Budget for maintenance: $800-$1,500/car/year
  • Bad-order cars: railroad will set out for repair
  • End-of-lease: car must meet return conditions
  • Document condition at lease start AND return

Measuring Performance & Scaling

What to measure, who to hire, and how to grow your rail program

Rail Program KPIs

KPI. Target. Why It Matters. Transit Time. <Plan by 10%. Inventory planning accuracy. On-Time %. >85%. Supply chain reliability. Car Dwell (hours). <24 hrs. Demurrage cost control. Demurrage Cost/Car. <$50 avg. Operational efficiency. Car Utilization. >80%. Fleet ROI. Cost per Ton-Mile. Track trend. Rate negotiation data. Claims Rate. <0.5%. Loading quality. CO2 per Ton-Mile. Track trend. ESG reporting.

Building Internal Rail Expertise

  • Dedicated rail logistics coordinator: the single point of accountability for your rail program
  • Ideal background: rail logistics operations, railroad operations, supply chain management, or military logistics
  • Key skills: railroad portal navigation, demurrage management, contract analysis, regulatory compliance
  • Training: this course + AAR/railroad-specific programs + on-the-job with railroad reps
  • At 50+ cars/month: consider a full-time rail manager with authority over fleet decisions
  • At 200+ cars/month: build a rail logistics team (manager + coordinator + billing analyst)
  • Alternative: outsource to a rail logistics provider (RSI, BNSF Logistics, UTLX) if volume doesn't justify headcount
  • Either way: SOMEONE must own rail performance metrics and be accountable for results

Rail Logistics Provider vs

In-House Rail Management. Use a Rail Logistics Provider When. Volume under 50 cars/month. Rail is new to your company (learning phase). You need rate benchmarking data fast. Multi-commodity, multi-railroad complexity. Short-term project freight (construction, seasonal). No internal rail expertise available. Go In-House When. Volume over 100 cars/month (savings justify headcount). Rail is core to your supply chain (not supplemental). You want direct railroad relationships and negotiating power. Long-term commitment to rail (5+ year horizon). You need real-time control over operations. Data and performance transparency matter.

Scaling Your Rail Program: The Growth Path

  • PHASE 1 (0-25 cars/month): Test the lane — use railroad-supplied or spot-leased cars, learn the basics
  • PHASE 2 (25-100 cars/month): Commit — sign a contract, lease a fleet, hire a rail coordinator
  • PHASE 3 (100-500 cars/month): Optimize — negotiate multi-year rates, invest in siding upgrades, track KPIs
  • PHASE 4 (500+ cars/month): Unit train potential — build/expand siding for 100+ car trains, lowest rates
  • Each phase unlocks rate discounts: railroads reward volume, consistency, and fast car turns
  • Don't skip phases — railroads evaluate your track record before offering premium rates
  • Invest in infrastructure at each phase: Phase 1 = basic siding, Phase 4 = unit train loop track
  • The jump from Phase 2 to Phase 3 is where most shippers see the biggest ROI improvement

12-Month Rail Implementation Plan

  • Q1 Months 1-3: Research & Setup
  • Complete modal conversion analysis
  • Contact railroads serving your facilities
  • Tour existing transload facilities
  • Identify car type and fleet size needs
  • Begin insurance and agreement paperwork
  • Q2 Months 4-6: Launch
  • Sign railroad contract and car leases
  • Build or upgrade rail siding if needed
  • Hire/designate rail coordinator
  • Set up tracking and demurrage tools
  • First test shipments (10-20 cars)
  • Q3 Months 7-9: Optimize
  • Review KPIs: dwell, transit, cost
  • Fix operational bottlenecks
  • Train loading crew on AAR standards
  • Negotiate demurrage credits if needed
  • Scale to full contracted volume
  • Q4 Months 10-12: Expand
  • Annual rate review and renegotiation
  • Evaluate adding lanes/commodities
  • Build sustainability reporting
  • Present ROI to leadership
  • Plan Phase 2 growth investments

Key Terms — Rail Strategy

  • Modal Conversion
  • Shifting freight from truck to rail — requires cost analysis and infrastructure investment
  • Side Track Agreement
  • Legal contract between shipper and railroad for use of a rail siding on your property
  • Unit Train. 100+ car train of a single commodity, cycling between origin and destination — lowest rates
  • Manifest Train
  • Mixed-commodity train assembled in classification yards — slower but more flexible
  • Revenue-to-Variable Cost ratio — STB uses this to determine if rates are 'reasonable'
  • Car Velocity
  • How quickly a railcar completes a trip cycle — railroads' #1 operating metric under PSR
  • National Industrial Transportation League — shipper advocacy group for rail issues
  • Track configuration allowing trains to load/unload without stopping — required for shuttle trains

Key Takeaways
