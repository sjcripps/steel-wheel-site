Rail Freight Glossary: 50+ Terms Every Shipper Should Know

May 14, 2026 · 13 min read · Reference
Bookmark this page: 50+ rail freight terms every shipper hears in carrier calls, on invoices, in contracts, and in trade press — defined in plain English by people who book and audit rail moves for a living. Organized by category so you can scan for the term you need.

Rail freight has its own vocabulary. Some of it comes from the operations side — the language of yards, crews, and trains. Some of it comes from the commercial side — tariffs, contracts, surcharges, and accessorials. Some of it comes from the regulators. None of it is hard, but all of it shows up the first time a shipper opens an invoice or sits down with a carrier rep, and a missing definition can cost real money.

This glossary covers the terms a shipper actually runs into — not every word in the dictionary, but the ones that show up in BOLs, rate sheets, contracts, and weekly operations calls. The definitions are written in the order a shipper is likely to encounter them in practice, then grouped by category for reference. If you are new to rail, read it top to bottom. If you are looking up one term, jump straight to the section.

Equipment and Railcars

Rail freight is an equipment business. The car type usually decides the rate, the loading method, and which lanes are even available. The terms below cover the cars and loading hardware a shipper is most likely to deal with. For a deeper visual walkthrough, our rail car types explained guide covers each car class in more detail.

  • Auto rack: Multilevel enclosed car for moving finished motor vehicles, typically two or three decks. Used almost exclusively by OEMs and their logistics providers.
  • Boxcar: Enclosed car with side doors, used for paper, packaged goods, lumber products, and other freight that needs weather protection but does not need bulk gravity unloading.
  • Centerbeam: Flatcar with a vertical center beam running its length, designed for banded loads of dimensional lumber, wallboard, and building materials.
  • Coil car: Specialized car with a trough and hoods for moving steel coils without damaging the wraps. Used heavily in steel and automotive supply chains.
  • Covered hopper: Enclosed bulk car with roof hatches for loading and bottom or pneumatic discharge. Standard for grain, plastic resin, fertilizer, sugar, flour, and other dry bulk that needs to stay clean and dry.
  • Flatcar: Open deck with no sides, used for machinery, lumber, pipe, steel plate, and as the base for multimodal container loading.
  • Gondola: Open-top car with low sides, used for scrap metal, slag, finished steel products, and aggregate that can ride uncovered.
  • Hopper (open-top): Bulk car with bottom-gate discharge, exposed to weather. Used for coal, frac sand, aggregates, and other commodities that can handle the elements.
  • Tank car: Cylindrical pressure or non-pressure car for liquids, gases, and liquefied solids. Pressure cars carry LPG, anhydrous ammonia, and similar; non-pressure cars carry petroleum products, chemicals, vegetable oils, and food-grade liquids.
  • Well car: Multimodal car with a recessed center well that allows containers to be stacked two high (double-stack). The workhorse of containerized rail.
  • AEI tag: Automatic Equipment Identification — the RFID tag on every North American railcar that wayside readers use to track equipment across the network.
  • Bad order: A car flagged as out of service for mechanical or structural defects, requiring repair before it can move again. Catching a bad order during your pre-loading inspection saves freight loss and demurrage downstream.

Operations and Service

Operations terminology describes how cars actually move — how they are sorted, blocked, handed off, and tracked. Most of these terms appear in carrier service updates, in waybill movement events, and in the conversations between operations teams when something goes wrong.

  • Block: A group of railcars that move together because they share an origin-destination routing. Building good blocks is the central efficiency game of rail operations.
  • Classification yard: A facility where freight cars are sorted by destination and reassembled into outbound trains. Hump yards use a small hill and gravity; flat yards rely on switch engines. Our classification yards guide walks through both.
  • Consist: The list of locomotives and railcars that make up a specific train, in order. Asking a carrier for the consist of an inbound train shows the cars and their position.
  • Drayage (dray): The local trucking move that connects a rail terminal or transload to the shipper or consignee's door. Often billed separately from the line haul.
  • Dwell: The time a railcar sits in a yard between movements. Average terminal dwell is a public Class I service metric, generally reported in hours.
  • In-gate / out-gate: The events when a container enters or leaves a multimodal terminal. The timestamps drive free-time clocks and chassis billing.
  • Interchange: The point where one carrier hands a car off to another. The full process — physical movement, documentation, liability transfer — has its own playbook, covered in our railroad interchange guide.
  • Manifest train: A mixed-freight train made up of cars from multiple shippers going to multiple destinations, sorted at classification yards along the way. The default service for shippers who do not generate full unit trains.
  • Multimodal: Freight that moves on more than one mode of transportation — typically a container or trailer that rides on rail for the long haul and a truck for the local move. The dominant growth category in US rail.
  • Run-through train: A train that does not break up at an interchange; it crosses from one carrier to another with the same consist and crew change instead of being re-sorted. Faster transit, when the lane supports it.
  • Switching: The act of moving cars within a yard or local area — pulling them out of a track, putting them onto another, building blocks, spotting them for loading.
  • Spot: To place a railcar at a specific location for loading or unloading. "We were spotted four covered hoppers this morning" means four cars were physically delivered to the customer's track.
  • Trip plan: The carrier's planned routing, blocking, and timing for a specific shipment. Service performance is often measured against trip-plan compliance.
  • Unit train: A single-commodity, single-shipper, single-destination train that bypasses classification yards. The most efficient service tier in rail — but it requires enough volume to fill the train. Compared in detail in our unit trains vs manifest service guide.
  • Velocity: Average miles per hour for trains in the network, reported by carriers as a service metric. Falling velocity is usually the first sign of network congestion.

Documentation and Codes

Rail freight runs on paperwork, even when the paperwork is electronic. The terms below cover the documents and codes that travel with every shipment. The full operational picture is in our rail freight documentation guide.

  • Bill of lading (BOL): The primary transport contract between shipper and carrier. The BOL identifies the shipper, consignee, commodity, weight, routing, and terms. Inaccurate BOLs are one of the most common sources of billing disputes.
  • Waybill: The carrier's internal routing and billing document, generated from BOL data. The electronic waybill is what actually moves through the carrier systems and triggers car movement events.
  • STCC code: Standard Transportation Commodity Code — a seven-digit code identifying the commodity being shipped. Wrong STCC equals wrong rate, wrong routing, and potential hazmat misclassification. Full explanation in our STCC codes explained piece.
  • Hazmat (DOT class): Regulated hazardous materials, classified by the US Department of Transportation into nine hazard classes. Hazmat shipments require shipping papers, placarding, and trained personnel on both ends.
  • Shipping papers: The hazmat-specific documentation set required to accompany a hazardous-materials shipment, including the proper shipping name, hazard class, UN/NA identification number, packing group, and emergency response information.
  • Consignee: The receiving party at destination — the entity to whom the freight is being delivered.
  • Consignor / shipper: The party originating the shipment.
  • Routing instructions: The list of carriers and interchange points the shipper has selected for the move. On contract shipments the routing is usually baked into the agreement.

Pricing and Charges

Most of the cost questions on a rail invoice come down to a handful of terms. The mechanics of how rates get built are covered in our how rail freight rates work guide; what shows up on the invoice is broken out in our rail freight invoice explained piece.

  • Line haul: The base charge for the railroad's movement of the car from origin terminal to destination terminal. The biggest single line item on most rail invoices.
  • Tariff: A published rate document filed by a carrier, listing rates and rules for moves that fall under the tariff. The default fallback when no contract is in place.
  • Contract rate: A negotiated, typically confidential rate agreed in a written contract between shipper and carrier. The vast majority of rail freight moves under contract rather than tariff.
  • Indicative estimate: A non-binding price approximation provided in advance of an actual move, typically used during evaluation, pilot planning, or budget development. Steel Wheel Logistics provides indicative estimates — we do not guarantee rates outside a signed agreement.
  • Accessorial: Any charge on the invoice that is not line haul or fuel — weighing, cleaning, switching, special permits, and similar.
  • Fuel surcharge (FSC): A separate charge that recovers the carrier's diesel cost, typically calculated off the Department of Energy On-Highway Diesel Fuel Price (HDF) index. Mechanics are detailed in our rail fuel surcharges piece.
  • HDF index: The Energy Information Administration's weekly retail diesel price benchmark — the most common index that rail and truck fuel surcharges reference.
  • Demurrage: A daily charge billed by the railroad when a railcar is held beyond the permitted free-time window at the customer's facility. Designed to keep equipment in revenue service. Full playbook in our demurrage fees guide.
  • Detention: A similar concept on the trucking side — a charge for holding a trailer or chassis at a facility beyond free time. Often confused with demurrage but technically distinct.
  • Free time: The period a shipper or consignee is allowed to hold a railcar before demurrage starts accruing — typically 24 to 48 hours, sometimes negotiable on contract lanes.
  • Per diem: A separate daily charge railroads bill each other for the use of one another's railcars. Mostly an inter-carrier issue, but it shows up in private-car economics.
  • Mileage allowance: Compensation paid by the railroad to the car owner (often a shipper or lessor) for the carrier's use of a private car on its system, calculated per loaded mile.
  • Reciprocal switching: A local move performed by one railroad to deliver or pick up a car from a customer whose track is reached through another carrier. The fee for that move is the switching charge.
  • Loaded mile / empty mile: The two halves of a car's journey. Loaded miles are revenue; empty miles are positioning cost. Lane balance is one of the structural drivers of rail pricing.
  • Indicative lane: A specific origin-destination pairing for which a non-binding estimate is offered. Try ours on the tools page for a sample lane quote.

Carriers and the Network

The North American rail network is a hierarchy. Knowing which carrier sits where in that hierarchy matters for routing, pricing, and service expectations.

  • Class I railroad: The largest US and Canadian carriers, defined by an annual operating revenue threshold set by the Surface Transportation Board. There are seven Class Is in North America: Union Pacific, BNSF, Norfolk Southern, CSX, Canadian National, CPKC, and the Ferromex/KCSM operations.
  • Class II railroad: Regional railroads — smaller than Class I but larger than the short lines. The middle tier.
  • Class III railroad: Short line and switching carriers. Roughly 600 short lines operate in the US, handling first and last miles of much of the network. Full comparison in our short line vs Class I piece.
  • Short line: A Class III railroad, typically operating in a defined region or set of branch lines, often serving as the connection between a Class I and the shipper's facility.
  • Switching carrier: A railroad whose primary business is moving cars within a terminal or local area, rather than line-haul transportation.
  • Origination carrier: The first railroad in a routing — the carrier that picks the car up from the shipper.
  • Delivering carrier: The last railroad in a routing — the carrier that delivers the car to the consignee's facility.
  • Rail logistics provider: A company that coordinates rail freight on behalf of shippers — rate negotiation, car sourcing, routing, transload coordination, documentation, and tracking. Steel Wheel Logistics is one. Our guide to choosing one covers the criteria that matter.
  • Private car: A railcar owned or leased by a shipper rather than the railroad. Common in chemicals, grain, and any high-volume specialty equipment lane.
  • Rail spur / siding: A short section of track connecting a customer's facility to the main rail network, allowing cars to be spotted directly at the loading or unloading point. Construction economics are covered in our private rail spur cost piece.
  • Transload: A facility where freight is transferred between rail and truck (or rail and barge) — used by shippers who do not have direct rail access. Full breakdown in our what is transloading piece.
  • PSR (Precision Scheduled Railroading): An operating philosophy adopted by most US Class Is in the late 2010s and 2020s, focused on running fewer, longer trains on tighter schedules to drive down operating ratio. Reshapes service patterns and has real implications for shippers.

Regulators and Industry Bodies

Rail freight in the US sits inside a framework of federal regulators and industry associations. The terms below are the ones that show up most often in carrier communications, rate disputes, and safety topics.

  • AAR: Association of American Railroads — the industry trade association that publishes the weekly carload and multimodal traffic data, sets interchange rules through its committees, and represents the carriers in Washington.
  • STB: Surface Transportation Board — the federal economic regulator for rail. The STB handles rate disputes, merger reviews, common carrier obligation enforcement, and reciprocal switching petitions.
  • FRA: Federal Railroad Administration — the safety regulator for rail. The FRA inspects track, equipment, operating practices, and hazmat handling, and writes the rules carriers must follow.
  • BTS: Bureau of Transportation Statistics — the federal statistics agency for transportation, publisher of Freight Facts & Figures and the data behind most modal-share numbers.
  • EIA: Energy Information Administration — the federal energy data agency. The EIA's diesel price index is the most common reference for rail and truck fuel surcharges.
  • DOT: Department of Transportation — the cabinet-level department overseeing transportation regulation. Sets hazmat classifications and rules through its Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA).
  • Staggers Act: The 1980 federal law that partially deregulated rail freight pricing, enabling the industry's recovery from near-bankruptcy. Cited in almost every long-run rail pricing discussion.
  • Carmack Amendment: The federal statute governing carrier liability for loss and damage of freight in interstate commerce. The basis for filing a rail freight claim.

None of this terminology is exotic. Most of it shows up in a normal week of running a rail program — on an invoice, in a service alert, in a contract clause. The goal is not to memorize it; the goal is to recognize it when it lands in front of you and know roughly what it means. If a term comes up that is not in this glossary and is not clear from context, ask the carrier or the logistics partner. The wrong assumption usually costs more than the awkward question.

One more piece of advice: Vocabulary shifts a little between carriers. Some Class Is call the same charge by different names. Some short lines use legacy terms the larger carriers have dropped. When you are working a new lane or a new carrier, ask the rep to walk you through their invoice line items before the first car ships — it is the cheapest way to avoid surprises later.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a unit train and a manifest train?

A unit train is a single train of one commodity moving for a single shipper from one origin to one destination — bypassing the classification yard and earning the lowest per-car rate available. A manifest train is a mixed-freight train carrying cars from many shippers to many destinations, sorted and re-sorted at yards along the way. Manifest service is the default for shippers moving a handful of cars at a time; unit trains require enough volume to fill a whole train, often 75 to 110 cars or more.

What is demurrage on a rail shipment?

Demurrage is a daily charge a railroad bills the shipper or consignee for holding a railcar at a facility beyond a permitted free-time window — usually 24 to 48 hours. The fee is structured to encourage fast loading and unloading so the carrier can put the equipment back in revenue service. Rates commonly run $75 to $300 per car per day in 2026, with rates often escalating after the first few days.

What is the difference between an indicative estimate and a binding quote?

An indicative estimate is a non-binding approximation of what a shipment will cost, given the origin, destination, commodity, volume, and current market conditions. A binding quote is a guaranteed price that the carrier or provider commits to honor. In rail freight, almost no one issues binding quotes on a one-off basis — pricing becomes contractually binding only when a written contract or tariff is in place. Steel Wheel Logistics provides indicative estimates only and does not guarantee rates outside a signed agreement.

What is STCC and why does it matter?

STCC stands for Standard Transportation Commodity Code, the seven-digit code used to classify every commodity moving on the US rail network. The STCC determines how a shipment is rated, routed, and regulated — using the wrong code can lead to misapplied rates, incorrect hazmat handling, and billing disputes. Shippers should confirm the correct STCC with their logistics partner before the first bill of lading goes out.

What is the difference between dray and line haul?

Line haul is the main railroad portion of the move — the carrier hauling the car from origin rail terminal to destination rail terminal. Dray (or drayage) is the local truck move between the rail terminal or transload facility and the shipper or consignee's door. A full rail-truck door-to-door move typically has at least one dray on each end, billed separately from the line-haul rate.
