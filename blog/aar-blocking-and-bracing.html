AAR-Approved Blocking and Bracing: Securing Freight for Rail

August 27, 2026 · 11 min read · Compliance
The short version: "AAR-approved" is not a certificate somebody hands you. It means your load was secured using a method the railroads already publish and accept — a specific figure in a specific rulebook — and that you named that method on the bill of lading. If no published figure covers your commodity, you need a written approval from the originating carrier before the car leaves your plant, not after an inspector stops it.

A shipper who has moved steel plate on trailers for fifteen years already knows how to strap a load down. Then the same plate goes on a flatcar, gets humped in a yard three days into the trip, and arrives with the stack walked six inches off center and a bulkhead bent. Nothing about the securement was careless. It was simply designed for the wrong set of forces. Rail blocking and bracing is a separate discipline with its own rulebooks, its own approval path, and its own liability structure — and the shipper, not the railroad, owns most of it.

What Blocking and Bracing Actually Is

Blocking and bracing is the system of dunnage, restraints, and tie-downs that keeps a load in the same position on a railcar from loading to unloading. Blocking stops movement by filling space and taking bearing — timber chocks against a coil, cradles under pipe, floor blocking nailed to a boxcar deck. Bracing resists movement by transferring force into the car structure — gates, uprights, strapping, and banding that tie the load to itself and to the car.

The distinction matters because the two fail differently. Blocking fails by crushing, splitting, or being undersized for the bearing area. Bracing fails by stretching, loosening, or pulling out of the anchor point. A load that arrives shifted usually tells you which one gave up first, and the fix is rarely "add more straps."

What does not need blocking and bracing: free-flowing bulk in the equipment designed for it. Grain in a covered hopper, aggregate in an open hopper, liquid in a tank car — the car contains the commodity, and securement is a matter of hatches, gates, and valve closures rather than dunnage. If you are shipping grain by rail or moving crushed stone in hoppers, this article is not your compliance problem. It becomes your problem the moment freight sits on a deck instead of in a hold.

Why Rail Forces Break Highway-Grade Securement

Highway securement is designed around braking and cornering. The federal performance standard for truck cargo securement is expressed as a fraction of cargo weight — on the order of 0.8 of the load's weight forward and 0.5 laterally and rearward — and it assumes those forces arrive smoothly through tires on pavement.

Rail applies force differently in three ways that matter:

  • Longitudinal shock from coupling. The interchange standard holds coupling speed to roughly four miles per hour, and that is the controlled case. The energy arrives through the draft gear as a sharp spike, not a gradual deceleration, and everything on the deck feels it.
  • Slack action in the train. A long train has inches of free play at every coupler. When the head end pulls or bunches, that slack runs in or out along the consist like a whip. Your car takes those events repeatedly, in both directions, for the whole trip.
  • Duration. A cross-country truckload is a matter of days. The same freight moving by rail is on the network for weeks, passing through multiple yards, each one a chance for another coupling event. Securement that loosens one sixteenth of an inch per shock has plenty of shocks available to it.

That last point is the one shippers underestimate. Rail securement is not designed for a worst-case single event. It is designed for a long series of moderate events without progressive loosening. This is also why a car whose lading cannot tolerate impact carries a do-not-hump restriction — the mechanics of which we cover in our guide to hump yards and classification yards.

The Three Rulebooks and Which One You Fall Under

Equipment type decides your rulebook, not commodity. Find your car first, then your commodity section inside it.

Your equipment Governing AAR reference What it covers
Gondola, flatcar, bulkhead flat, centerbeam Open Top Loading Rules Manual (OTLR) General rules plus a commodity section — coil steel, pipe, lumber and forest products, structural shapes, machinery, and more
Boxcar, refrigerator car Closed Car Loading Rules, pamphlet series Floor blocking, dunnage, void fill, doorway protection, unitized loads
Non-pressure and pressure tank cars AAR Pamphlet 34 Loading and unloading procedure, fittings and valve securement, closure verification
Covered hopper, open hopper No blocking and bracing rule applies Gate and hatch closure, load level, and commodity contamination controls instead

Inside the Open Top Loading Rules, the general rules come first and the commodity sections follow. The general rules are the part people skip and the part that decides most disputes: they set what counts as acceptable dunnage material, how anchors may be applied, what the car itself must be capable of, and what happens when two rules appear to conflict. Read the general section before you read your commodity figure.

Access is the practical obstacle. The full manuals are gated to AAR and Railinc members, and most shippers are neither. In practice you work from the copy your serving railroad provides, from the excerpts your carrier's damage prevention group sends when you ask, or through a rail logistics provider who already has the references. That access question is worth resolving before you quote a customer on breakbulk freight, not after you win the business. What "AAR-Approved" Actually Means

AAR-approved blocking and bracing means the securement matches a published, accepted method — not that a body inspected your particular car and blessed it. There are two ways a load qualifies.

  1. It matches a published figure. The loading rules are drawings, not prose. A figure shows the commodity on a specific car type, with dimensions for the blocking, sizes and counts for the strapping, nailing patterns, and weight limits. If your load looks like the figure and stays inside its stated limits, it is approved by definition. Most freight lands here.
  2. It carries a specific written approval. When the commodity, the dimensions, or the car does not fit any figure, the load moves only on a documented approval from the originating carrier, usually written for one commodity, one car type, and sometimes one lane. It is not a general license, and it expires or lapses when the parameters change.

What "approved" is not: a certificate, a badge, a number you can look up, or something a dunnage vendor can grant you. A supplier who tells you their product is "AAR approved" is describing the material meeting a specification — that is not the same as your method being approved for your load. Those are two different questions and only the second one keeps the car moving.

One more trap worth naming. Staying inside a figure means staying inside all of its limits. Figures carry weight caps, dimensional caps, and often a stated car requirement. A load that follows the drawing exactly but is four tons over the figure's limit is not an approved load, and the mechanical inspector who catches it will treat it as an unapproved one.

Getting a Method Approved When No Figure Fits

Oversize fabrications, one-off machinery, unusual castings, and prototype equipment all end up here. The path is more ordinary than shippers expect, and the biggest mistake is starting it too late.

  1. Go to the originating railroad first, not to the AAR. Your serving carrier's damage prevention or loading engineering group is the entry point. They review against the general rules and they are the party who has to accept the car.
  2. Submit real drawings and real numbers. Commodity, total weight, weight distribution, center of gravity height, overall dimensions, the car type you intend to use, and a dimensioned drawing of the proposed securement. A sketch and a verbal description will come back as a request for drawings and cost you a week.
  3. Expect a validation requirement on anything heavy or novel. New methods are commonly proven by instrumented impact testing, where a loaded car is run into standing cars at stepped speeds and the securement is inspected between runs. The carrier tells you whether your load needs it.
  4. Get the approval in writing, and read its scope. Approvals name the commodity, the car type, the weight range, and frequently the specific move. Note what triggers a re-approval — a different car series, a heavier version of the same part, or a route change can all do it.
  5. Put the approval where the crew will find it. The loading crew needs the drawing at the dock and the reference belongs on the bill of lading. An approval sitting in someone's email while the crew loads from memory is worth nothing.

Budget real lead time. This is a design review inside a railroad, and it moves at the speed of one. Shippers who plan blocking and bracing at the same time they plan the rate rarely have a problem; shippers who plan it the week the freight is ready almost always do. Certification for Rail Freight Consignments: Who Certifies What

There is no third-party certification for a rail freight consignment. Certification on a rail move is a self-certification by the shipper, recorded on the bill of lading, and it is why "who loaded the car" is the most consequential detail on the document.

Three separate certifications can appear on a single move:

  • The securement reference. Language on the special instructions block naming the rule the load complied with — the standard phrasing is that the car was loaded and braced in accordance with a specific AAR rule and section. This is the securement certification.
  • The hazmat shipper's certification. For hazardous materials, the certification required by 49 CFR 172.204, which is a separate statement covering proper classification, description, packaging, marking, and labeling. Securement of hazmat packages inside the car is separately mandated by 49 CFR 174.55. Our hazmat rail shipping guide covers the wider set.
  • Shipper load and count. The notation that the shipper, not the carrier, loaded and counted the lading. Nearly every industry-loaded car is an SLC move, and it moves the burden of proof onto you when a claim gets filed — see shipper load and count versus carrier load for what that actually shifts.

The railroad's role is verification, not certification. Mechanical inspectors check loads against the rule cited, and a load that does not match gets held, restowed, or refused. They do not issue you a document saying the load passed. Absence of an objection is the only confirmation you get, which is precisely why the burden of proof runs the direction it does when something goes wrong.

Practical consequence: the rule you cite on the bill of lading is the standard you will be measured against. Citing a section you did not actually load to is worse than citing nothing — it converts a securement problem into a documentation problem with your signature on it.

The Four Failures That Cause Most Shifted Loads

Shifted loads are boring in their causes. Four patterns cover most of them.

  • Wrong dunnage material. Green or wet lumber that shrinks in transit, timber that splits under bearing load, or blocking sized off habit instead of off the figure. Blocking that is a quarter inch short on day one is an inch short after a week of shocks.
  • Under-tensioned or over-tensioned banding. Loose banding lets the load start moving, and once it moves it keeps moving. Over-tensioned banding cuts into corner protection, relaxes, and ends up loose anyway.
  • Voids nobody filled. Empty space in a boxcar between the load and the wall, or between load segments, is the single most common closed-car failure. The load does not need much room to build momentum.
  • A car that was not fit to load. Damaged floors, missing tie-down anchors, bent bulkheads, and debris in the car all defeat correct securement applied on top of them. Inspecting the car before you load it is a five-minute step that prevents an expensive one — our railcar inspection checklist covers what to look at.

Notice that three of the four are decided before the first strap goes on. Securement quality is mostly a materials and preparation problem, not a technique problem.

What a Securement Failure Actually Costs

The damaged freight is often the smallest line on the bill. A shifted load triggers a chain of costs that keep running while the problem gets solved:

  • The set-out. The car comes out of the train at the nearest place it can be handled. It stops moving until it is restowed.
  • The restow. Somebody has to re-secure the load, usually a contractor working at a location you did not choose, at a price you did not negotiate.
  • The clock. The car keeps accruing time while it sits, and your inbound plan at destination is now wrong. If the delay pushes free time at the destination, demurrage follows.
  • The claim you will probably lose. On a shipper-loaded car where the securement did not match the cited rule, a carrier liability claim is a hard argument. Understanding where that line sits is the difference between filing and eating it — our guide to filing a rail freight claim covers the evidence you would need.
  • The relationship. Carriers track shippers who generate load defects. It shows up later in how flexible they are on equipment supply and service.

A Pre-Release Securement Checklist

Run this before the car is released, not after:

  1. Identify the governing rulebook by car type — open top, closed car, or Pamphlet 34.
  2. Find the specific figure for the commodity and confirm your load is inside every limit it states, including weight.
  3. If nothing fits, start the carrier approval now and treat the lead time as part of the shipment schedule.
  4. Inspect the car — floor, anchors, bulkheads, doors, cleanliness — and reject it if it cannot hold the securement.
  5. Verify dunnage against the spec, especially lumber moisture and dimension.
  6. Photograph the completed load from both sides and both ends before the doors close or the car leaves. This is the cheapest evidence you will ever buy.
  7. Render the securement reference on the bill of lading, and make it the rule you actually loaded to.
  8. Apply handling restrictions where the lading needs them and skip them where it does not, since each one costs handling time.

Shippers moving fabricated metal see this list most often, since almost every steel product on an open-top car falls under a securement figure — our guide to steel and metals rail shipping goes deeper on the equipment side. The compliance framework around all of it, including hazmat and FRA requirements, is covered in the safety and compliance module of our free rail logistics course.

None of this is exotic. It is a design review, a materials spec, and a document reference, done in that order and done early. The shippers who struggle with blocking and bracing are almost never the ones who found it difficult — they are the ones who found it late. If you have freight that does not obviously match a published figure, that is a conversation to have while the lane is still being priced, and it is a normal part of what we handle for shippers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does AAR approved blocking and bracing mean?

It means the load was secured using a method the AAR publishes or has accepted, rather than one the shipper invented. For most commodities that method already exists as a figure showing exactly how the load sits on the car and how it is blocked, braced, and tied down. Loading to that figure, within all of its stated limits, is what makes a load approved. If no figure fits, you need a specific written approval before the car moves.

Which AAR rulebook applies to my railcar?

Equipment decides it. Open-top cars — gondolas, flatcars, bulkhead flats — fall under the Open Top Loading Rules Manual, with a section per commodity family. Boxcars and other closed cars fall under the Closed Car Loading Rules pamphlet series. Non-pressure and pressure tank cars fall under Pamphlet 34. Hoppers carrying free-flowing bulk need no blocking and bracing, because the car itself contains the commodity.

Is there a certification for rail freight consignments?

There is no third-party certificate you buy for a rail load. Certification on a rail consignment is a shipper self-certification made on the bill of lading, stating the car was loaded and braced in accordance with a named AAR rule. Hazmat adds the separate shipper's certification under 49 CFR 172.204. The railroad verifies by inspection rather than by issuing a document, and the shipper carries the liability.

How do I get a new loading method approved?

Start with the originating railroad's damage prevention or loading engineering group, not with the AAR directly. Submit the commodity, weight, dimensions, center of gravity, intended car type, and a dimensioned drawing of the securement. Heavy or novel loads are commonly validated by instrumented impact testing before acceptance. What you get back is a written approval scoped to a specific commodity, car type, and often a specific move.

Why is rail securement stricter than truck securement?

The forces come from a different direction and repeat far more often. Highway securement is designed around braking and cornering. A railcar takes repeated longitudinal shocks from coupling and from slack running in and out of the train, across a trip measured in weeks rather than days. A load that would ride a trailer without moving can walk itself loose on a flatcar in a single yard.

What happens if my load shifts in transit?

The railroad sets the car out at the nearest point it can be handled safely, and the load must be restowed before it moves again. You pay for the restow, absorb the delay, and the car keeps accruing time. If the securement did not match the rule cited on the bill of lading, a damage claim against the carrier becomes very hard to win, because the shipper loaded and certified the car.

Can my dunnage supplier certify my load?

No. A supplier can certify that its material meets a specification, which is a different question from whether your loading method is approved for your commodity and car. Approval attaches to the method, not to the products used in it. Only the published figure or a written carrier approval settles the method question.
