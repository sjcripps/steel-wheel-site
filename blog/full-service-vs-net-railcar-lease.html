Full-Service vs Net Railcar Lease: What's Included and What Each Really Costs

September 12, 2026 · 12 min read · Cost & Strategy
Before you sign for cars, find out what the freight underneath them prices at. Get an indicative rail freight quote for your lane in seconds — free, no signup.
The short version: A full-service railcar lease is a rental with a maintenance department attached. A net lease is closer to financing a piece of equipment you happen not to own. The monthly rates are not comparable numbers, and shippers who treat them as comparable pick the wrong one roughly every time.

Two quotes land for the same 4,750-cubic-foot covered hopper, five-year term, same delivery window. One is $615 a month. The other is $395. The instinct is to take the $395 and ask what took the other lessor so long. That instinct has cost shippers more money than any other decision in railcar leasing, because the two numbers are not measuring the same thing — one of them is a price for a railcar, and the other is a price for a railcar plus everything it takes to keep a railcar legal and rolling.

What Each Structure Actually Means

A full-service railcar lease bundles the equipment with the work required to keep it in service: routine and running maintenance, AAR interchange compliance, periodic regulatory qualification, shop routing, and in most cases the ad valorem property taxes assessed on the car. You pay one number per car per month and you largely stop thinking about the mechanical side of the fleet.

A net railcar lease conveys use of the car and very little else. Maintenance, inspections, regulatory requalification, property tax filings, registry administration, and every day the car spends sitting at a repair shop instead of earning are yours. The rate is materially lower — sometimes 30 to 40 percent lower on the same car — because the lessor has handed you the part of the business that is hard to price.

You will also hear triple net, finance lease, and per diem lease. Triple net is a net lease with taxes and insurance explicitly pushed to the lessee, which is how most net leases are written anyway. A finance lease is a net lease structured so you effectively buy the car over the term. A per diem lease is a short-term daily rental, priced accordingly. For comparison purposes there are two real products, and the rest are variations on the net side.

Line by Line: What Full-Service Covers

Full-service means the lessor owns the maintenance obligation, not that nothing can ever be billed to you. Here is what a standard full-service agreement puts on the lessor's side of the line.

Cost item Full-service lease Net lease
Running repairs (wheels, brakes, draft gear) Lessor Lessee
AAR interchange compliance Lessor Lessee
Regulatory requalification (tank cars) Lessor Lessee
Shop routing and empty movement to shop Lessor (usually) Lessee
Ad valorem / property tax Lessor, built into rate Lessee, filed by state
Reporting marks, UMLER, registry fees Lessor Lessee or lessor, negotiated
Liability insurance Lessee Lessee
Damage you caused Lessee Lessee
Cleaning between commodities Lessee (typically) Lessee

The regulatory qualification row is the one that decides most tank car leases. Tank cars carry a periodic requalification cycle under 49 CFR 180.509 — interior and exterior visual inspection, thickness testing, service equipment work, and where the specification calls for it, pressure testing. Intervals depend on the car's spec and what it hauls, and the current due dates are stenciled on the car. That event is expensive, it is scheduled by the calendar rather than by your production plan, and it takes the car out of service for weeks. On a full-service lease it is the lessor's problem and their scheduling headache. On a net lease it is a capital event you have to forecast, fund, and cover with a spare car.

What Full-Service Does Not Cover

Full-service is not all-inclusive, and the exclusions list is where the disagreements happen. Assume every one of the following is billed back to you regardless of structure:

  • Damage from your operation. Gates torn off by a front-end loader, a hatch cover backed into, a car overloaded past its stenciled load limit.
  • Commodity damage. Corrosion or lining failure caused by loading a product the car was not specified for. Load the wrong thing once and the repair can exceed a year of rent.
  • Cleaning. Switching commodities generally means a cleaning bill on your side, plus the empty move to a cleaning facility.
  • Modifications you requested. Gate conversions, added lining, specialty fittings — and often the cost of putting the car back to standard at return.
  • Demurrage and storage. The lessor does not care that the car sat; the railroad bills you. Our guide to rail demurrage fees covers how those clocks run.
  • Return condition shortfalls. The gap between how you hand the car back and the standard in the return clause.
Read the exclusions, not the brochure. Two full-service quotes at the same rate can differ by thousands of dollars per car per year depending on whether "running repairs" includes wheelsets, whether the lessor pays for empty movement to shop, and how narrowly "abuse" is defined.

What You Take On With a Net Lease

The lower net rate buys you a set of obligations that are easy to underestimate because most of them are administrative rather than mechanical. In practice a net lessee owns six functions:

  1. Mechanical. Someone has to know when a car is bad-ordered, where to send it, which shop has capacity, and whether the estimate is reasonable. That is either a person on your staff or a fleet manager you pay.
  2. Regulatory calendar. Qualification and inspection due dates per car, tracked ahead of time, with cars cycled to shops before they come due rather than after.
  3. Car repair billing. Reviewing what railroads and shops bill against your cars, disputing what is wrong, and paying what is right.
  4. Property tax. Ad valorem taxes on private cars are apportioned across the states the car actually operated in. Full-service lessors do this at scale. On a net lease, you file.
  5. Registry. Keeping the UMLER record accurate at Railinc, paying registry fees, and updating the record whenever the car's configuration changes.
  6. Spare capacity. A car in the shop is a car not loading. Net lessees need either a spare or a plan, and both cost money.

None of these is hard on its own. Collectively they are a job, and the question is not whether you can do it — you can — but whether you do it for less than the rate spread. Shippers running a handful of cars generally do not, because the lessor is spreading the same overhead across tens of thousands of cars and buying shop time in volume.

Repairs: Owner vs Handling-Line Responsibility

One thing neither structure changes is that not every repair on a private car is paid by whoever holds the lease. The AAR Interchange Rules split repairs into two buckets, and the split holds regardless of who your lessor is.

Owner-responsibility items are normal wear — wheels worn to condemning limits, brake shoes, routine component life. The car's owner pays, which on a full-service lease means the lessor and on a net lease effectively means you.

Handling-line responsibility items are damage caused by a railroad while the car was in its possession. The handling carrier pays. These are billed through the industry's car repair billing system administered by Railinc, with a billing repair card generated for the work.

The practical difference between structures is not who is theoretically liable — it is who chases the money. A full-service lessor has a department that reads every repair card, catches items miscoded as owner responsibility when the car was damaged in a carrier's yard, and disputes them. A net lessee who does not do that work simply pays. That recovery function is invisible in the rate comparison and is worth real dollars on an active fleet.

How to Compare the Two Rates Honestly

The only defensible comparison adds a maintenance-and-compliance estimate to the net quote before you look at either number. Do it in this order:

  1. Start with the net monthly rate. That is your floor, not your cost.
  2. Add annualized running repairs, divided by 12. Ask the lessor for the car's repair history if it is existing equipment. If it is new, use a planning figure and flag that it rises sharply in the back half of the car's life.
  3. Add the amortized qualification event. Take the expected cost of the next requalification or program work and spread it across the months until it comes due. On tank cars this line alone often closes most of the spread.
  4. Add property tax and registry administration. Small dollars, real hours.
  5. Add the cost of downtime. Days out of service multiplied by what a loaded car earns you, or the cost of the spare you keep to cover it.
  6. Now compare. If the adjusted net number is not meaningfully below the full-service quote, full-service is buying you certainty for free.

For context on where the full-service side of that comparison sits, published planning ranges for grain-service covered hoppers have run roughly $530 to $635 per month, with C-114 grain cars in the low $600s on five- to seven-year terms. Those are full-service numbers. Separately, GATX's Lease Price Index showed renewal rates repricing about 26.7 percent above expiring leases in Q2 2026 — which means anyone whose lease is coming up should budget for a step change rather than a rollover. The Clauses That Move More Money Than the Rate

Once you have the structure right, four clauses decide whether the deal ages well.

Mileage allowance and credits

Private cars can earn mileage allowance from railroads under certain tariffs. Who keeps it is a lease term, not a law. Full-service lessors usually retain it because it is already priced into your rate; net lessees more often keep it. Get the answer in writing — over a multi-year term on a high-mileage lane it is not a rounding error.

Return conditions

The return clause is the single most underestimated line in a railcar lease. It sets the condition the car must be in when you hand it back: cleaned to a stated standard, free of your product, lining intact, qualification dates with a stated minimum remaining, modifications reversed. A vague return clause is an open-ended bill at the end of the term. Negotiate it at signing, when you have leverage, not at expiration when you do not.

Term and renewal

Full-service terms commonly run one to five years; net leases skew five to ten because the economics behave like financing. Short terms carry a premium in every market, and renewal pricing resets to whatever the market is doing when your term ends. Build the renewal risk into the decision — a cheap five-year rate signed into a rising market is a very different asset than the same rate signed into a falling one.

Substitution and utilization

Can the lessor swap equipment on you? Does the lease carry a minimum utilization or mileage commitment with a penalty attached? Is there a fleet-flex provision that lets you drop cars if volume falls? Structure matters here: net lessors rarely give flexibility because the deal is a financing, while full-service lessors sometimes will, at a price.

Which Structure Fits Which Shipper

Full-service fits shippers running fewer than roughly 25 to 50 cars, anyone without a mechanical function on staff, tank car programs where the qualification cycle is the dominant cost, first-time car lessees, and any operation where budget predictability is worth more than the last few points of cost. This is most shippers, and it is not a failure of sophistication — it is a correct reading of where their overhead is best spent.

Net fits fleets large enough to support a real fleet-management function, operations that already run shop relationships and repair-billing review, long stable programs where the term risk is acceptable, and shippers who want ownership economics without the balance sheet. If you are already reviewing repair cards and tracking qualification dates on cars you own, the marginal cost of adding net-leased cars is genuinely small.

There is a third path worth naming: a mixed fleet. Core baseload volume on net-leased or owned cars where utilization is predictable, and seasonal or surge volume on full-service or short-term cars where it is not. That structure is common among shippers who have been at this a while, and it is usually built after a bad year taught them what a fixed fleet costs when demand drops. Whichever way you go, run the comparison on total cost, not on the rate line. A $395 car that needs $180 a month of maintenance, tax, and downtime is a $575 car, and it is a $575 car that calls you at three in the afternoon when it is bad-ordered in a yard six states away.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a full-service and a net railcar lease?

A full-service lease bundles the car with maintenance, AAR and regulatory compliance work, and usually ad valorem tax filing into one monthly rate. A net lease gives you the car and almost nothing else: repairs, requalification, taxes, registry administration, and the days the car spends in a shop are yours. Full-service costs more per month and far less in surprises.

Is a net railcar lease actually cheaper?

Only if you can run the maintenance and compliance function for less than the spread between the two rates. The net rate is lower on paper, but you inherit running repairs, periodic requalification, ad valorem tax filings in every state the car operates, and the revenue lost while the car sits at a shop. Small fleets almost never beat a lessor's blended cost; large fleets with a mechanical staff sometimes do.

Does a full-service railcar lease cover all repairs?

No. Full-service covers normal wear and regulatory maintenance. Damage you cause, contamination from your product, overloading, corrosion from a commodity the car was not lined for, and modifications you requested are billed back to you. Read the exclusions list, not the marketing sheet, because that list is where full-service leases stop being all-inclusive.

Who pays ad valorem taxes on a leased railcar?

On a full-service lease the lessor normally files and pays the state property tax apportioned by where the car ran, and it is built into your rate. On a net lease that obligation is typically yours, which means registering and filing in every state your car operated in during the year. It is a small dollar item with a large administrative tail.

Who keeps the mileage allowance credits on a leased railcar?

It depends entirely on the lease, and it is one of the most commonly missed negotiating points. Full-service lessors usually retain mileage allowance earned from railroads because it is already priced into the rate. On net leases the lessee more often keeps it. Ask the question in writing before you sign, because over a long term the credits can move real money.

How long are railcar leases?

Full-service leases commonly run one to five years, with shorter terms available at a premium. Net leases skew longer, often five to ten years, because the economics look more like financing the equipment than renting it. Term length is one of the biggest single drivers of the rate you are quoted.
