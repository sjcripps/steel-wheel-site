How to Avoid and Dispute Rail Demurrage Fees: A Shipper's Playbook

March 26, 2026 · 12 min read · Cost & Strategy
Key fact: The Surface Transportation Board received over 1,800 comments on its 2019 demurrage rulemaking — more than on almost any other freight rail issue. Shippers are paying more in demurrage than ever, and many of those charges are disputable.

Demurrage fees are one of the biggest controllable costs in rail freight. They're also one of the most misunderstood. Plenty of shippers treat demurrage invoices like utility bills — they show up, you pay them, you move on. That's a mistake. A significant portion of demurrage charges stem from railroad-caused delays, billing errors, or policy applications that don't hold up under scrutiny. If you're not auditing every invoice and disputing the ones that don't add up, you're leaving money on the table. This guide covers the math behind demurrage costs, the operational changes that actually reduce charges, and a step-by-step process for disputing fees when the railroad is at fault.

The Real Cost of Demurrage: Running the Numbers

Demurrage looks manageable when you're thinking about one car. A hopper sitting an extra day at $100 — that's a rounding error on a $5,000 freight move. The problem is that demurrage never stays at one car for one day. It compounds across your fleet, and escalating rate tiers make every additional day exponentially more expensive.

Most Class I railroads use a tiered rate structure. The first day or two past free time might cost $75-$150 per car. Days three through five jump to $150-$250. Beyond day five, you're often looking at $200-$300+ per car per day. A single covered hopper that sits for a week past free time doesn't cost $700 — it costs $1,200 to $1,600 once you account for the escalation.

Now multiply. If you're moving 80 cars a month and averaging 1.5 excess days per car — which is common for shippers without a dedicated car management process — your annual demurrage bill runs somewhere between $144,000 and $216,000. That's not a minor line item. That's a salary, a piece of equipment, or half a year's worth of freight rate savings from choosing rail over truck in the first place.

Three Cost Scenarios Every Shipper Should Model

Before you can fix demurrage, you need to know what it's actually costing you. Most shippers have never modeled this. Here are three scenarios to benchmark against — and once you've read them, our demurrage calculator will let you drop your real numbers into the same framework.

Scenario 1: The Disciplined Shipper

50 cars per month, average 0.3 excess days per car, base rate $100/day. Annual cost: roughly $18,000. This shipper has a car manager, pre-stages product, and releases cars within an hour of completion. Demurrage exists but it's controlled — mostly caused by occasional equipment issues or weather delays that are partially recoverable through disputes.

Scenario 2: The Average Shipper

50 cars per month, average 1.5 excess days per car, blended rate $120/day (accounting for some tier escalation). Annual cost: roughly $108,000. This shipper works cars when they get to them, releases at end of day, and doesn't track free time proactively. About 30% of their charges are potentially disputable but they don't file because the process feels opaque.

Scenario 3: The Overwhelmed Shipper

50 cars per month, average 3.5 excess days per car, blended rate $175/day (heavy escalation). Annual cost: roughly $367,000. This shipper has limited track capacity, no dedicated car management, orders cars before product is ready, and doesn't release promptly. Half their charges involve constructive placement because their tracks are always full. They pay every invoice without review.

The gap: The difference between a disciplined shipper and an overwhelmed one — same volume, same railroad — is $349,000 per year. That's not a cost of doing business. That's a process failure with a price tag.

The Demurrage Avoidance Checklist

Demurrage prevention comes down to a handful of operational habits executed consistently. None of this is complicated. The hard part is doing it every day, for every car, without exception. If you're looking for a primer on what demurrage is and how rates work, start there — this section assumes you know the basics and want to stop bleeding money.

1

Audit Your Ordering Discipline

Pull your last 90 days of car orders and compare them against your actual daily loading/unloading capacity. If you're consistently ordering more cars than you can work in a day, you're building demurrage into your process by design. Your order cadence should match your throughput — not your optimism about throughput.

2

Track Every Car's Free Time Clock

Use your railroad's shipper portal (BNSF ShipperConnect, UP Customer Technology, CSX ShipCSX, NS AccessNS) to monitor placement times in real time. Set alerts at 50% of free time consumed — not at the deadline. By the time you hit 100%, it's too late to avoid the charge.

3

Pre-Stage Product and Documentation

Product should be at the loadout before the empty car arrives. Warehouse space should be cleared before the loaded car is spotted. Bills of lading should be pre-filled with everything except the car number and seal. Every minute between car placement and the start of work is free time you'll never get back.

4

Release Cars Immediately — Not at End of Day

The moment a car is loaded or unloaded, submit the release electronically. Batching releases until the afternoon or the next morning is one of the most common sources of avoidable demurrage. A car that's physically done at 10 AM but not released until 4 PM has burned six hours for no reason. On a car approaching its free time limit, those six hours are the difference between zero charges and a full day of demurrage.

5

Run a Weekly Demurrage Review

Every week, pull your demurrage report from the railroad portal. Review every charge. Categorize each one: shipper-caused, railroad-caused, weather, or unknown. The shipper-caused ones tell you where your process is failing. The railroad-caused ones go into the dispute pile. The unknowns need investigation. If you can't explain a charge, you can't prevent it from recurring.

Fixing Facility Bottlenecks That Drive Charges

Sometimes the problem isn't process discipline — it's physical infrastructure. If your facility can only hold 6 cars but the railroad is spotting 10, you're going to accrue constructive placement charges no matter how fast your crew works.

Track Capacity

Map your actual track capacity against your average inbound car volume. If you're regularly exceeding capacity, you have three options: expand your trackage (expensive, long lead time), negotiate arrival windows with the railroad to spread deliveries across the week, or reduce your per-order car count and increase order frequency. The third option costs nothing and solves the problem for most shippers.

Equipment Reliability

A loader that breaks down twice a month doesn't just slow production — it creates demurrage exposure on every car waiting behind it. Track your equipment downtime and correlate it with demurrage charges. If a single piece of equipment is responsible for 40% of your excess dwell time, the repair or replacement cost almost certainly pays for itself in avoided demurrage within a few months.

Crew Scheduling

Cars don't stop burning free time on weekends, but many facilities stop working them. If you receive cars Friday afternoon and your crew doesn't return until Monday, that's 48+ hours of free time consumed with zero productivity. Staggering crew schedules to cover weekend car work — even a partial shift — can eliminate a major source of recurring charges.

How to Dispute Demurrage: Step by Step

Filing a demurrage dispute isn't adversarial — it's a normal part of the shipper-railroad relationship. Railroads expect disputes, have teams dedicated to processing them, and routinely grant full or partial reversals when the documentation supports it. The shippers who never dispute aren't being good partners. They're being inattentive.

1

Identify Disputable Charges

Review your demurrage invoice line by line. Flag any charge where the delay was caused by the railroad rather than your operation. Common disputable scenarios: the railroad failed to spot or pull cars on schedule, delivered a car with mechanical defects that prevented loading, placed a car at the wrong track, or delivered multiple cars simultaneously that were supposed to arrive across several days (bunching).

2

Gather Your Documentation

For each disputable charge, compile: the car number and reporting mark, actual placement and release dates/times from the railroad portal, your internal records showing when the car was worked, the specific cause of the delay, and any supporting evidence — photos of equipment defects, switching delay records, weather reports, or email correspondence with local railroad personnel about service issues.

3

File Within the Window

Each Class I railroad has a dispute filing deadline — typically 30 to 90 days from the invoice date. Miss this window and you lose your right to dispute regardless of the merits. BNSF and UP generally allow 90 days. CSX and NS have tighter windows. Check your railroad's tariff or contract for the specific deadline, and build a calendar reminder to file well before it expires.

4

Submit a Clear, Factual Dispute

Your dispute should state the car number, the date range in question, the dollar amount disputed, and a specific factual explanation of why the charge is invalid. Avoid vague language like "service was poor." Instead: "Car BNSF 462817 was placed at Track 4 at 08:15 on March 3. Per our standing order, this car should have been spotted at Track 2. Re-spotting was completed at 22:40 on March 3, consuming 14.4 hours of our 48-hour free time due to a railroad placement error."

5

Track and Follow Up

Log every dispute with a reference number, date filed, amount, and resolution status. If you haven't received a response within 30 days, follow up. Railroads process thousands of disputes — yours can fall through the cracks if you don't stay on it. Maintain a running dispute log so you can identify patterns: if the same switching crew or the same yard consistently causes charges, that's leverage for a broader conversation with your railroad account rep.

Building a Dispute Case That Wins

The strength of a demurrage dispute comes down to specificity and documentation. Railroads deny vague claims. They grant specific ones. Here's what separates a successful dispute from a rejected one.

Equipment Defects

A hopper car arrives with a jammed discharge gate. Your crew can't unload it until the railroad sends a mechanical team, which takes 18 hours. That's 18 hours of free time lost to a railroad equipment problem. Photograph the defect, note the time you reported it, and record when the repair was completed. This is one of the highest-success-rate dispute categories because the fault is unambiguous — you can't unload a car that doesn't work.

Switching Failures

You released a car at 06:00 Tuesday. The railroad didn't pull it until 14:00 Thursday — 56 hours later. During that time, the car was technically "released" but still sitting on your track, blocking other cars from being spotted. The eight-hour pull shouldn't have been 56 hours. Request switching records from the railroad showing when your release was processed and when the car was actually pulled. The gap is your dispute.

Car Bunching

Your contract calls for three cars per week, spread across Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. The railroad delivers all three on Monday morning. Your facility can only work one car per day, so cars two and three sit for one and two extra days respectively — burning free time because the railroad didn't deliver on schedule. This is a strong dispute, especially if you have a written agreement or historical pattern showing the expected delivery cadence.

Constructive Placement Disputes

The railroad starts your free time clock on a car sitting in their yard because your tracks are full. But your tracks are full because the railroad hasn't pulled three cars you released two days ago. The constructive placement charge is a direct result of the railroad's own switching delay. Document the release times of the cars blocking your tracks, the railroad's failure to pull them, and the resulting inability to receive the new car. This connects the railroad's delay to your inability to accept placement — a causal chain that supports a dispute.

Record-keeping rule: Log every car placement, every release, every equipment defect, and every switching delay — with timestamps and names. The shippers who win disputes are the ones with better records than the railroad. If you can't prove it happened, it didn't happen as far as the dispute process is concerned.

The STB's Role in Demurrage Reform

The Surface Transportation Board has taken an increasingly active role in demurrage policy. In 2020, the STB issued a final rule establishing principles for demurrage and accessorial charges. The core principle: demurrage should incentivize efficient railcar use and rail network fluidity — not serve as a revenue source for penalizing shippers for delays they didn't cause.

The STB's guidance specifically addresses several shipper pain points. Railroads should not charge demurrage for delays caused by their own service failures. Constructive placement charges should account for whether the railroad contributed to the track congestion. Free time policies should be transparent and consistently applied. These aren't legally binding mandates with enforcement mechanisms, but they establish the regulatory framework that the STB uses when evaluating complaints.

For shippers, the practical takeaway is this: if your railroad's demurrage practices feel unreasonable — if you're being charged for delays the railroad caused, if constructive placement is being applied unfairly, if rate increases seem disconnected from any service improvement — you have a regulatory avenue. The STB accepts formal complaints, and even the act of filing one often motivates the railroad to negotiate. A rail logistics provider with experience navigating railroad tariffs and STB processes can help you determine whether a formal complaint is warranted.

Negotiating Demurrage Terms in Your Rail Contract

If you're shipping under a contract (as opposed to tariff rates), demurrage terms are negotiable. Most shippers don't push on demurrage during contract negotiations because they're focused on the per-car rate. That's shortsighted. A contract with a great freight rate but standard demurrage terms can end up costing more than a contract with a slightly higher rate and favorable demurrage provisions.

Extended Free Time

The standard 48 hours of free time works for facilities with fast turnaround and ample track capacity. If your operation legitimately needs more time — maybe you're a smaller facility that can only work one car per day, or your commodity requires slow loading procedures — negotiate for 72 or 96 hours of free time. Railroads will often grant extended free time in exchange for volume commitments or guaranteed car cycle times on the back end.

Rate Caps on Escalation

Even if you can't avoid all demurrage, you can negotiate caps on the escalation tiers. A contract provision that caps demurrage at the Tier 1 rate regardless of dwell time protects you from the worst-case scenarios. Railroads may resist this, but if your volume is significant and your historical turnaround is reasonable, it's a negotiable point. For more on how rail contracts and rate negotiations work, check our first-time shipper guide.

Reciprocal Penalties

Some shippers have successfully negotiated reciprocal demurrage — where the railroad pays the shipper a credit for every day it fails to spot or pull a car within an agreed service window. This is harder to get but increasingly common in high-volume contracts. It creates symmetry: if you owe the railroad for holding their cars, they owe you for delays on their end. The credits typically offset future demurrage charges rather than resulting in cash payments, but the financial impact is real.

Constructive Placement Protections

Push for contract language that limits constructive placement charges when the track congestion is caused by the railroad's failure to pull released cars. A standard provision might read: "Constructive placement shall not apply when shipper has released cars that remain on the facility track awaiting railroad switching." This single clause can eliminate one of the most frustrating and common sources of disputed charges.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I dispute a rail demurrage charge?

File a written dispute with the railroad's demurrage department within 30-90 days of the invoice date. Include the car number, placement and release dates, the specific cause of the delay, and supporting documentation. Each Class I railroad accepts disputes through their shipper portals.

What percentage of demurrage disputes are successful?

Success rates depend on documentation quality and fault attribution. Disputes citing clear railroad fault — equipment defects, switching failures, car placement errors — have significantly higher success rates. Shippers with detailed daily records typically recover 30-50% of disputed charges.

Can the STB help with unfair demurrage charges?

Yes. The Surface Transportation Board's 2020 final rule established that demurrage should incentivize efficient car use, not penalize shippers for railroad-caused delays. Shippers can file formal complaints with the STB if a railroad's demurrage practices are unreasonable.

How much can demurrage cost per year for a mid-size shipper?

A shipper handling 100 railcars per month averaging 1.5 excess days per car at $100/day faces roughly $180,000 annually. With escalating rate tiers, the real number is often higher. Shippers with poor turnaround discipline can easily exceed $300,000-$500,000 per year.

What is constructive placement and can I dispute it?

Constructive placement means the railroad starts your free time clock even though the car hasn't been physically spotted at your facility — typically because your tracks are full. You can dispute these charges if the railroad caused the backup by failing to pull released cars or by delivering cars off schedule.
